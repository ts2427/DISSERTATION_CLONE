### Article N18 — NEW ⭐
**Wang, H.E., Wang, Q.E., & Wu, W. (2022).** Short selling surrounding data breach announcements. *Finance Research Letters*, *47*, 102690.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finlet.2022.102690
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1544612322000198
**SSRN preprint:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3554487
**Published:** Finance Research Letters, Vol. 47, 2022

**Summary:** Using a large U.S. sample, the study documents significantly negative cumulative abnormal returns (CARs) around data breach announcements and finds that on a cross-sectional basis, short-selling activity strongly predicts the magnitude of CARs. Firms with higher pre-announcement short interest experience steeper post-announcement price declines, consistent with informed trading. The study further provides evidence that short-selling activity improves market quality and accelerates price discovery, implying that information asymmetry between informed short sellers and the broader market is systematically elevated around breach events.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** Central contribution — documents that informed short selling drives price discovery around breach announcements, providing a direct mechanism linking data breaches to H5's information-asymmetry proxies (bid-ask spread, trading volume, short interest). Extendable to test whether the FCC's 2024 mandatory-disclosure rule compresses the short-selling informational advantage.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** Cross-sectional analysis shows short-selling effects vary with firm characteristics, consistent with H1's prediction that firm size, credit quality, and governance moderate market reactions.
- **H2 (Regulation impact):** The short-selling channel provides a mechanism through which faster mandatory disclosure could reduce informed-trader advantage, making this paper directly relevant to H2's theoretical framing.
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** The strong cross-sectional effect of prior breach history on CARs (noted by the authors) complements H3 evidence.

### Article N19 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Obaydin, I., Xu, R., & Zurbruegg, R. (2024).** The unintended cost of data breach notification laws: Evidence from managerial bad news hoarding. *Journal of Business Finance & Accounting*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/jbfa.12794
**URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jbfa.12794
**Published:** Journal of Business Finance & Accounting, 2024 (Wiley Online Library)

**Summary:** Using the staggered adoption of U.S. state-level data breach notification (DBN) laws as a quasi-natural experiment, the paper finds that DBN laws paradoxically *increase* future stock price crash risk. The mechanism is managerial bad-news hoarding: faced with mandatory disclosure requirements, managers strategically delay or accumulate unfavorable news to avoid triggering mandatory reporting obligations, until the burden of accumulated bad news crosses a threshold and crashes the stock. The effect is most pronounced in firms with higher information asymmetry, weaker governance, and greater financial constraints — the very firms regulation is intended to protect.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ Highest-priority paper for H2 this week. Provides *causal evidence* (staggered-adoption quasi-experiment) that mandatory disclosure regulation produces unintended market consequences. Directly addresses the H2 mechanism: regulation does not simply reduce information asymmetry — it may shift the timing and form of bad-news release, affecting return distributions.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** The bad-news hoarding mechanism is an information-asymmetry amplifier — disclosure mandates can increase, rather than decrease, information gaps in the pre-crash accumulation period.
- **H3 (Repeat breach / crash risk):** Firms that hoard bad news across multiple breach events face compounding crash risk, supporting H3's prediction of escalating market penalties.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** The heterogeneous effect by governance quality and financial constraints is directly relevant to H1's firm-characteristic moderation hypothesis.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Highest-priority new citation this week for H2.* Published in a top-tier accounting/finance journal (ABDC A; ABS 3). Pair with Cao et al. (2024, IRFA) and Block (2025, arXiv) to triangulate the regulatory-impact literature for H2. The quasi-experimental design is methodologically superior to event studies for causal identification of H2.

### Article N20 — NEW ⭐
**Martins, A.M., Moutinho, N., & Cró, S. (2025).** Stock market effects of major cyber-attacks: evidence for breached and cybersecurity listed firms. *Journal of Banking Regulation*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1057/s41261-025-00293-y
**URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41261-025-00293-y
**Published:** Journal of Banking Regulation (Springer Nature), September 2025

**Summary:** Event-study analysis of 53 major global cyberattacks finds statistically significant negative abnormal returns for breached firms concentrated around disclosure dates, while cybersecurity-sector firms experience significant *positive* abnormal returns around the same events — consistent with demand for security services rising after high-profile breaches. For breached firms, the negative reaction is amplified when: (1) the firm is small, (2) leverage is high, (3) growth opportunities are elevated, and (4) the breach involves financial information loss. These results span a cross-country sample, adding generalizability beyond U.S.-only studies.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** The four cross-sectional moderators (size, leverage, growth, info type) directly map to H1's firm-characteristic hypotheses. Provides out-of-sample international confirmation of U.S.-centric H1 findings.
- **H4 (Breach type differential effects):** Financial-information breaches produce the strongest negative reactions, consistent with H4's prediction that data-type severity (financial > credential > operational) drives differential market responses.
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** The paper references that firms with prior cyberattack history face higher negative reactions, providing cross-country corroboration of H3.
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** The positive cybersecurity-sector spillover provides a natural counterfactual for H5 — information released about breached firms simultaneously increases demand signals for the security sector.
- **H2 (Regulatory context):** Published post-FCC/SEC rule implementation; cross-country variation in disclosure regimes offers a comparative angle for H2.

### Article N21 — NEW ⭐
**Muktadir-Al-Mukit, D., & Ali, M.H. (2025).** The dynamics of stock market responses following the cyber-attacks news: Evidence from event study. *Information Systems Frontiers*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10796-025-10639-6
**URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10639-6
**Published:** Information Systems Frontiers (Springer Nature), August 6, 2025

**Summary:** Using a dataset of 776 U.S.-listed firm cyber-attack incidents between 2012 and 2022, the event study documents an average market-value loss of $309.33 million on the day a cyber-attack is reported, with the negative response intensifying when news appears in high-credibility media sources. The study identifies severity of the incident, distinctiveness of the attack, data-privacy concerns, and industry-level cyber-risk exposure as key cross-sectional moderators. The large sample (776 incidents over 10 years) provides substantial statistical power and allows sub-period analysis bracketing major regulatory shifts (pre/post GDPR, pre/post state-level DBN laws).

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** Industry cyber-risk exposure and firm-level severity measures are cross-sectional moderators, consistent with H1. The media-credibility effect adds a disclosure-channel dimension.
- **H4 (Breach type):** Severity, privacy sensitivity, and distinctiveness of attack type all produce differential market reactions — directly supports H4's multi-type taxonomy.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** Media credibility as an amplifier of market reaction implies that information-quality differences between announcement channels create asymmetric investor responses.
- **H2 (Regulation):** Sub-period analysis spanning 2012–2022 allows before/after regulatory-event tests (state DBN adoption, SEC rules) within a large, well-powered sample.
- **FLAG:** Large-N design (776 incidents) makes this a methodological reference for dissertation power calculations.

### Article N22 — NEW ⭐
**Demek, K.C., & Kaplan, S.E. (2023).** Cybersecurity breaches and investors' interest in the firm as an investment. *International Journal of Accounting Information Systems*, *49*, 100616.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.accinf.2023.100616
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1467089523000088
**Published:** International Journal of Accounting Information Systems, Vol. 49, 2023

**Summary:** An experimental study examining how two factors — (1) whether a firm pre-breach indicated that cybersecurity risk management is a strategic initiative, and (2) whether the CEO issues a post-breach apology — affect nonprofessional investors' investment interest. The findings show that CEO apology significantly improves investors' investment impression and perceptions of CEO affective and cognitive trust. Importantly, investment interest is *lowest* when the firm had previously claimed cybersecurity was a strategic priority but the CEO fails to apologize — the gap between stated commitment and post-breach behavior creates a trust penalty.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H6 (Executive turnover response):** The CEO apology effect provides a behavioral-science complement to the turnover literature: executives who fail to communicate effectively post-breach face the highest stakeholder-trust penalty, creating board-level pressure for turnover. Directly enriches H6's theoretical mechanism.
- **H2 (Disclosure signaling):** The "stated cybersecurity commitment" variable maps directly to H2 — mandatory disclosure rules force firms to make cybersecurity commitments public (e.g., via SEC Form 8-K Item 1.05), raising accountability expectations and the cost of post-breach communication failures.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Governance signaling (cybersecurity-as-strategy framing) is a firm-characteristic moderator of investor reaction, consistent with H1's governance-quality channel.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** CEO communication quality post-breach mediates information asymmetry between management and investors — supports H5's mechanism linking disclosure quality to market outcomes.

### Article N23 — NEW ⭐ [2021 — WITHIN PRIMARY WINDOW]
**Hartmann, B.M., & Carmenate, T. (2021).** Academic research on the role of corporate governance and IT expertise in addressing cybersecurity breaches: Implications for practice, policy, and research. *Current Issues in Auditing*, *15*(2), A9–A25.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.2308/CIIA-2020-034
**URL:** https://publications.aaahq.org/cia/article/15/2/A9/7096/Academic-Research-on-the-Role-of-Corporate
**Published:** Current Issues in Auditing (American Accounting Association), Vol. 15, Issue 2, Fall 2021

**Summary:** A structured review of the academic literature on how corporate governance mechanisms and executive IT expertise jointly affect firms' cybersecurity breach outcomes. Key synthesis findings: (1) CIO turnover is significantly elevated following breaches caused by system deficiencies (CIO accountability domain), but not for breaches caused by criminal fraud or human error; (2) CEOs are more likely to turn over following both system-deficiency and human-error breaches, reflecting the CEO's broader organizational accountability; (3) board IT expertise and CISO board presence materially reduce the incidence of reportable breaches; and (4) audit committee characteristics (financial expertise, independence) moderate the breach-reporting timeliness and accuracy.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** Core reference — the breach-type-conditioned turnover finding (system deficiency → CIO/CEO departure; fraud → no CIO turnover) provides a granular theoretical prediction for H6 beyond simple breach-occurrence effects. Directly informs the dissertation's H6 empirical design.
- **H1 (Firm governance characteristics):** Board composition (IT expertise, CISO presence) as a governance-quality moderator of breach outcomes is a firm-characteristic variable relevant to H1's cross-sectional heterogeneity.
- **H4 (Breach type):** The breach-type conditioning of executive accountability (system deficiency vs. criminal fraud vs. human error) complements H4's breach-type taxonomy for differential market and governance effects.
- **H2 (Regulation):** Audit committee reporting requirements and mandatory disclosure rules are discussed as policy levers — the paper bridges governance and regulatory literatures.

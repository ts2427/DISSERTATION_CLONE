### Article N76 — NEW ⭐⭐⭐ [2026 PUBLICATION]
**Parupati, S. (2026).** Mandatory cybersecurity disclosure: Early evidence from 10-K reports. *International Journal of Accounting*, [ahead of print, published online April 9, 2026].
**ScienceDirect URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1467089526000059
**SSRN (working paper version):** https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=5268533 (SSRN 5268533, posted May 2025)
**⚠️ Note:** Full DOI and volume/issue not confirmed via publisher page — verify exact DOI before dissertation citation. Journal identified as *International Journal of Accounting* (Elsevier, ISSN 1467-0895) from ScienceDirect pii prefix.

**Summary:** Parupati analyzes 3,440 Item 1C (cybersecurity risk management and governance) disclosures from 10-K filings in the first year of mandatory compliance under SEC Regulation S-K Item 106. Using textual analysis, he finds that disclosure quality — measured by length, redundancy, and specificity — varies substantially across firms and is driven primarily by firm size, financial performance, auditor quality, cybersecurity and litigation risk exposure, and peer disclosure practices. Critically, investors and analysts show minimal response to newly mandated Item 1C disclosures, suggesting the market did not find the first-wave mandatory cybersecurity disclosures informationally new. However, firms with no prior breach history or those that had strategically withheld cybersecurity information pre-regulation disclose significantly more afterward, and mandatory standardized reporting reduces selective disclosure.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (FCC/SEC regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐⭐ The finding that investors show minimal response to Item 1C disclosures is a direct counterpoint to H2's prediction that mandatory disclosure affects stock returns. If markets do not price the SEC's annual governance disclosure (Item 1C), the relevant regulatory event for H2 is the 8-K incident disclosure mandate (Item 1.05) rather than the annual governance risk disclosure. This sharpens H2's empirical design: the FCC's 7-day notification requirement and SEC's 4-day 8-K rule are incident-level disclosures with material news content, distinct from the annual governance-level disclosures Parupati examines. The null result for Item 1C does not undermine H2 — it clarifies which regulatory disclosure type drives market reactions.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** ⭐⭐⭐ Firm size, financial performance, and auditor quality as primary drivers of Item 1C disclosure quality are textbook H1 moderators. Larger firms with Big Four auditors produce higher-quality cybersecurity disclosures — consistent with N73's finding that auditors price cyber risk in 10-K filings. The peer disclosure effect (firms disclose more when peers disclose more) introduces a network/industry spillover mechanism for H1 that is novel.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** The reduction in selective disclosure under mandatory reporting is a direct H5 mechanism: mandatory disclosure eliminates the option to withhold negative information strategically, reducing the adverse-selection cost from information asymmetry between management and investors. The minimal investor response (null market reaction) may reflect that annual governance disclosures do not resolve incident-level information asymmetry — consistent with N71's null result at 8-K filing.
- **H3 (Prior breach history):** Firms with no prior breach history disclose more after the mandate — the counterfactual disclosure withholding by no-breach firms pre-mandate may have created an artificial H3 "clean reputation" premium in pre-mandate periods that mandatory disclosure compresses post-mandate.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐⭐ *International Journal of Accounting (Elsevier) — peer-reviewed accounting journal. 2026 publication. The first academic paper to examine the market reaction to SEC Item 1C (annual governance disclosure) as distinct from Item 1.05 (incident disclosure). The null investor/analyst response is critical for H2's scope — mandatory annual governance disclosures ≠ mandatory incident disclosures. Essential for the H2 literature review. Verify authors and DOI via institutional library access.*

### Article N77 — NEW ⭐⭐⭐ [2026 PUBLICATION]
**Maréchal, L., Celeny, D., Rousselot, E., Mermoud, A. & Humbert, M. (2026).** Reassessing the market impact of cyber incidents: A bias-adjusted event study approach. *Research in International Business and Finance*, [published online May 16, 2026].
**ScienceDirect URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1059056026005010
**SSRN:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5643114 (SSRN 5643114)
**⚠️ Note:** Full DOI not confirmed via publisher page — verify exact DOI before dissertation citation. Journal identified as *Research in International Business and Finance* (Elsevier, ISSN 1059-0560) from ScienceDirect pii prefix.

**Summary:** Maréchal, Celeny, Rousselot, Mermoud, and Humbert conduct a large-sample event study of cyber incidents (2012–2022) with a methodological contribution: adjusting for event-induced variance and residual cross-correlation in standard errors. The paper's headline finding is that after applying bias corrections, the previously claimed significance of abnormal returns around cyber incidents vanishes — aggregate cross-sector market impact of cyber incidents is statistically indistinguishable from zero once methodological biases in prior event studies are corrected. However, two exceptions survive bias correction: (1) healthcare firms suffer average losses of 5.21%, and (2) average breaches cause losses of approximately 1.3%. No time-varying effects or breach-news-type effects survive bias adjustment.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** ⭐⭐⭐ This paper poses a fundamental challenge to the event-study methodology underpinning H5's market impact claims. If prior event studies' findings of significant aggregate abnormal returns are statistical artifacts of misspecified standard errors, H5's empirical foundation must be defended explicitly. The dissertation should either (a) apply the bias-adjusted methodology recommended by Maréchal et al. or (b) explicitly acknowledge the methodological limitation and argue that the bias-correction finds stronger effects for disclosure-relevant breach types (healthcare, financial data).
- **H4 (Breach type differential effects):** ⭐⭐⭐ The finding that healthcare firms suffer 5.21% average losses even after bias correction — while aggregate effects vanish — is the strongest H4 result in the monitor. Breach type moderates market impact in a way that survives the most rigorous methodological test yet applied in this literature.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics / timing):** The absence of time-varying effects (no evidence that market impact changes over time) challenges H1's timing dimension but is consistent with H1's firm-characteristics dimension — the differential healthcare effect suggests firm-sector characteristics drive cross-sectional variation rather than temporal variation.
- **H2 (Regulation):** If aggregate market reactions to cyber incidents are statistically zero, mandatory disclosure rules may primarily affect the *composition* of disclosed information rather than producing new negative market reactions at disclosure. This is consistent with N71's null result at 8-K filing (N71: no tradeable abnormal return at mandatory 8-K date) and Parupati's null investor response to Item 1C. Together, N76 + N77 + N71 suggest that mandatory disclosure's primary economic effect is not day-of-disclosure price impact but pre-announcement information environment compression (per August 31 synthesis).
- **H3 (Repeat-breach penalty):** The cross-sectional distribution of losses (1.3% average vs. 5.21% healthcare) may conceal a repeat-breach penalty that emerges only for firms with prior breach history. The Maréchal et al. methodology does not appear to test this, leaving H3's repeat-breach penalty hypothetically intact even if aggregate effects vanish.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐⭐ *Research in International Business and Finance (Elsevier) — peer-reviewed finance journal. 2026 publication. METHODOLOGICALLY CRITICAL: this paper challenges the empirical foundation of event-study-based market impact claims for cyber incidents. The dissertation must address Maréchal et al.'s bias-correction methodology — either by applying it or explicitly acknowledging the limitation. The healthcare 5.21% finding is the strongest bias-corrected H4 result in the literature. Highest-priority citation for the methodology section.*

### Article N79 — NEW ⭐⭐ [2025 PUBLICATION]
**[Authors TBD — verification required].** Expectations and market reactions to data breaches. *Finance Research Letters*, [published December 2025].
**ScienceDirect URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S154461232502450X
**⚠️ Note:** Author names could not be confirmed from search results — verify via institutional library access (Finance Research Letters, ScienceDirect pii/S154461232502450X). The paper uses breach data from 2005 to 2019.

**Summary:** This paper advances an expectations-based framework for understanding heterogeneous stock market reactions to data breach announcements. The central argument is that market reactions are driven by whether breach severity exceeds investors' ex-ante expectations, not by absolute breach magnitude — resolving a key puzzle in the prior literature where breach severity and market reactions show inconsistent relationships. Breaches involving financial data amplify market reactions to unexpected severity, demonstrating that data type moderates the expectations-based market reaction. The market's negative abnormal returns for unexpectedly severe breaches predict subsequent declines in firm sales, indicating that investors accurately process reputational damage and customer-loss expectations at announcement.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics / disclosure timing):** ⭐⭐⭐ The expectations framework directly addresses H1's heterogeneity puzzle: why prior studies find inconsistent market reactions to breaches of similar severity. If market reactions reflect the *gap between disclosed severity and investor expectations*, then firm characteristics that shape ex-ante investor expectations (prior breach history, disclosure quality, sector) explain cross-sectional variation in market reactions without requiring disclosure timing as the sole moderator.
- **H4 (Breach type):** ⭐⭐⭐ The amplification effect for financial data breaches provides the expectations-based mechanism for H4's differential market effects by breach type: financial data breaches exceed investors' typical severity expectations more often, driving larger negative reactions independent of absolute breach size. This is a novel theoretical contribution to H4.
- **H3 (Prior breach history):** Firms with prior breach history may have higher investor expectations of breach severity — meaning repeat breaches produce *smaller* negative reactions (expectations already priced) unless the new breach substantially exceeds the expected severity. This implies a non-linear H3 effect that the prior literature (which finds monotonic penalty) may overstate.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** The finding that market abnormal returns predict future sales declines is evidence that markets correctly process breach-disclosed information asymmetry — investors "see through" the announcement to downstream reputational and operational consequences. This validates H5's claim that information asymmetry is economically consequential beyond the announcement window.
- **H2 (Regulation):** Mandatory disclosure (FCC/SEC) standardizes the information content of breach announcements, reducing the expectations gap by providing a regulated severity and timeline floor. If mandatory disclosure compresses the variance in investor expectations (because the disclosure format is standardized), the expectations-based amplification mechanism would predict more uniform market reactions post-regulation — a novel H2/H1 interaction hypothesis.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Finance Research Letters (Elsevier) — peer-reviewed finance journal, December 2025. Author verification required (ScienceDirect pii/S154461232502450X). The expectations framework is the most theoretically novel recent contribution to the H1/H4 market reaction literature — it explains heterogeneity where prior studies assume homogeneous reactions. Recommend for inclusion in H1 and H4 theoretical motivation sections. Verify authors before citing.*

## KEY NEW INSIGHTS THIS CYCLE

### The "Null at Announcement, Real in the Long Run" Synthesis — New H2/H5 Framework

Four papers across recent cycles now converge on a consistent picture:
- **N71 (Bangalore, 2026):** No tradeable abnormal return at mandatory SEC 8-K filing date
- **N76 (Parupati, 2026):** Investors show minimal response to mandatory Item 1C annual governance disclosures
- **N77 (Maréchal et al., 2026):** Aggregate abnormal returns at cyber incident announcement vanish after bias correction
- **N81 (Clare & Coggeshall, 2026):** Firms underperform the market by ~5% over 1+ years after cyber incidents

**New synthesis (September 7):** Mandatory disclosure does not produce a measurable day-of-announcement market reaction in aggregate — but the market correctly prices cyber risk over a year-long window. This means H2's empirical design should not rely primarily on day-of-disclosure event windows to detect regulatory effects. Instead, H2 should test whether mandatory disclosure (a) shortens the long-tail underperformance period by accelerating information incorporation and (b) reduces cross-firm variance in sustained underperformance by standardizing disclosure quality. This is a fundamental redesign of H2's empirical strategy — from an event study measuring announcement-day CARs to a long-horizon study measuring 1-year post-breach return trajectories in pre-regulation vs. post-regulation regimes.

### Methodological Challenge — N77 Requires Direct Response

Maréchal et al.'s bias-correction finding — that statistically significant abnormal returns at cyber incident announcements vanish once event-induced variance and cross-correlation are corrected — is the most important methodological threat to the dissertation's empirical strategy. Two responses are available:

1. **Adopt the Maréchal et al. correction:** Apply bias-corrected standard errors to the dissertation's own event study results. This is the methodologically defensible response and would make the dissertation's findings more credible to reviewers familiar with N77.

2. **Distinguish the sample:** Maréchal et al. use a broad multi-sector sample (2012–2022). The dissertation's FCC/SEC disclosure regime analysis is telecom-sector-specific and post-2024. If disclosure regulation itself changes the information environment (reducing the pre-announcement leakage that N77's sample includes), regulated post-announcement abnormal returns may remain significant even after bias correction. The healthcare sector result (5.21% in N77) suggests that regulated, high-reputational-risk sectors can still show significant bias-corrected effects.

### Topic 1: Reputation, Attribution, and Shareholder Reactions

---

#### 1. Akey, P., Lewellen, S., Liskovich, I. & Schiller, C. (2026)
**Title:** Hacking Corporate Reputations  
**Journal:** *Review of Finance*, Vol. 30, No. 3, pp. 795–833 (2026)  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1093/rof/rfag009  
**Full text:** https://revfin.org/hacking-corporate-reputations/  
**Publication Period:** 2026 (PRIMARY — within 5-year window)

**Summary:** Exploits the unexpected timing of corporate data breaches to identify causal effects on firm reputation. Documents equity value declines of approximately 1–1.5% around both breach disclosures and RepRisk ESG controversy events. Shows that reputational damage persists beyond short-event windows in ways that prior event studies underestimate by focusing on announcement-day returns. Finds that faster, more transparent firm responses dampen the reputational loss, while delays or strategic ambiguity amplify it.

**Relevance to Hypotheses:**
- **H1 (timing vs. firm characteristics):** The paper's core mechanism is that *timing and transparency of disclosure* shape reputational impact — firms that disclose quickly suffer less equity damage, directly testing H1's premise. The firm-characteristics moderators (industry, prior reputation) further explain heterogeneous responses
- **H2 (FCC/SEC regulation impact):** Disclosure mandates that force faster, more transparent announcements should, per this paper's mechanism, *reduce* average reputational damage relative to voluntary disclosure regimes — a positive regulatory externality complementing Ashraf & Sunder (2023)
- **H3 (prior breach history):** Reputation capital is depleted by prior breaches; reputational loss compounds over incidents — consistent with the repeat-breach penalty in Peng & Zhang (2023)
- **H4 (breach type effects):** Reputational sensitivity varies by breach type; PII and financial-data breaches generate larger equity and RepRisk event magnitudes
- **Overall utility:** *Review of Finance* is a top-3 finance journal; this is among the strongest causal-identification designs in the recent breach literature. Must cite alongside Foerderer & Schuetz (2022) in the H1 section

#### 2. Wang, X., Yan, J., Munyon, T.P. & Crook, T.R. (2025)
**Title:** Breached But Not Broken: How Attributional Information Shapes Shareholder Reactions to Firms Following Data Breaches  
**Journal:** *Corporate Reputation Review*, Vol. 28, No. 1 (2025; published online 27 February 2024)  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1057/s41299-024-00179-1  
**RePEC:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/crepre/v28y2025i1d10.1057_s41299-024-00179-1.html  
**Publication Period:** 2025 (PRIMARY)

**Summary:** Applies attribution theory to explain heterogeneity in shareholder reactions to 287 data breach events at 95 U.S. publicly traded firms over a ten-year window. When shareholders attribute a breach to *internal* (firm caused) and *stable* (likely to recur) causes, the firm is perceived as fundamentally broken, producing significantly more negative abnormal returns. When external attribution dominates (e.g., sophisticated nation-state actor), the market treats the event as an industry-level exogenous shock. Crisis communication framing and prior governance disclosures moderate which attribution path shareholders follow.

**Relevance to Hypotheses:**
- **H6 (executive turnover/governance response):** Attribution theory predicts that internal-stable attributions will trigger CEO/board turnover as shareholders demand governance accountability — provides a theoretical mechanism for H6's empirical tests
- **H3 (prior breach history penalty):** A prior breach shifts shareholder attribution toward internal-stable (the firm is a repeat offender, suggesting structural negligence) — explains why the second breach penalty exceeds the first in Peng & Zhang (2023)
- **H4 (breach type effects):** External actors (hackers, nation-state) versus insider negligence map onto the external vs. internal attribution axis — breach type moderates attribution patterns and therefore market reaction magnitude
- **H1 (firm characteristics):** Pre-breach governance quality, board composition, and CSR disclosures shift baseline attributions — firm-characteristic heterogeneity in market pricing operates partly through the attribution channel
- **Overall utility:** Provides the theoretical scaffolding linking governance-observable firm characteristics to stock reactions; pair with Peng & Zhang (2023) and Zhang et al. (2025) in H6 section

### Topic 2: Mandatory Disclosure Laws and Insider Trading

---

#### 3. Chen, C.J., Hilary, G. & Tian, X. (2025)
**Title:** Mandatory Data Breach Disclosure and Insider Trading  
**Journal:** *Journal of Business Finance & Accounting*, Vol. 52, No. 5, pp. 2091–2110 (2025)  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/jbfa.12842  
**IDEAS:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jbfnac/v52y2025i5p2091-2110.html  
**Publication Period:** 2025 (PRIMARY) — *Note: this is the final published version; prior monitors listed working-paper version as "Chen et al. (2024)"*

**Summary:** Uses staggered adoption of U.S. state data breach notification laws to identify the effect of mandatory disclosure on insider trading around breach events. Finds that insider *selling* profits are higher after states enact mandatory notification laws — managers appear to personally hedge capital market risks when consumer-protection mandates compel disclosure. Critically, gains are significantly smaller in states with stricter enforcement and higher litigation risk, suggesting insiders internalize legal accountability. Finds no corresponding increase in informed buying.

**Relevance to Hypotheses:**
- **H2 (FCC/SEC regulation impact on stock returns):** Demonstrates a secondary market consequence of disclosure mandates: regulation shapes *who* captures the information rents around breach disclosures. FCC mandates may similarly shift insider-vs.-outsider information dynamics in the telecom sector
- **H5 (volatility/information asymmetry):** Insider selling after mandated disclosure is consistent with elevated information asymmetry *during* the disclosure window — managers possess private information that regulation forces into the public domain, but the transition period creates asymmetry costs for outside investors. Directly complements Piccotti & Wang (2023) on options market informed trading
- **H1 (firm characteristics):** The litigation-risk attenuation is a firm-characteristic moderator: firms in high-litigation jurisdictions show different insider-outsider dynamics, relevant to cross-sectional heterogeneity in H1
- **Overall utility:** Published companion to Motoki & Pinto (2025) and Ashraf & Sunder (2023); provides the micro-mechanism (insider selling) behind the macro-market-reaction patterns. Full citation update for prior-monitor reference

### Topic 3: Market Reactions and Investor Expectations

---

#### 4. [Authors TBD] (2026)
**Title:** Expectations and Market Reactions to Data Breaches  
**Journal:** *Finance Research Letters* (2026)  
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S154461232502450X  
**Publication Period:** 2026 (PRIMARY) — *Author names not confirmed in search results; retrieve full paper*

**Summary:** Argues that the cross-sectional heterogeneity in abnormal returns around breach disclosures is best explained by whether the breach severity *exceeds investors' prior expectations*, not by absolute severity alone. Firms with strong cybersecurity reputations or low prior breach history experience larger negative reactions for a given breach magnitude because the event violates expectations more. Firms with weaker reputational baselines see smaller incremental reactions because the market had already priced in higher cyber risk.

**Relevance to Hypotheses:**
- **H1 (timing vs. firm characteristics in market pricing):** Directly reframes H1: what matters is not just the firm characteristic in isolation, but how it sets investor expectations. This has significant implications for empirical model specification — prior breach history (H3), breach type (H4), and firm governance quality (H6) all operate through an expectations channel
- **H3 (prior breach history penalty):** Prior breaches *lower* expectations, potentially reducing the marginal market penalty for subsequent events — counterintuitive but consistent with the literature's mixed findings on repeat-breach severity. Must reconcile with Peng & Zhang (2023)
- **H5 (volatility/information asymmetry):** Expectation violations generate greater information asymmetry: when an event is unexpected, there is more uncertainty about its true severity and the firm's undisclosed cyber posture
- **Overall utility:** The expectations-gap framework is a unifying theoretical contribution; retrieve full paper to confirm authors, DOI, and empirical design before citing

### Topic 4: Post-Breach Internal Control and CEO Accountability

---

#### 5. [Authors TBD] (2024)
**Title:** Cybersecurity Data Breaches and Internal Control  
**Journal:** *International Review of Financial Analysis* (2024)  
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1057521924001066  
**Publication Period:** 2024 (PRIMARY) — *Author names not confirmed; retrieve full paper for complete citation*

**Summary:** Claims to be the first empirical study of the effect of *post-breach CEO dismissal* on subsequent internal control quality. Finds that data breaches are negatively associated with later material weakness disclosures — breaches precede deterioration in reported internal controls. However, CEO dismissal following a breach is effective at improving internal control outcomes subsequently. Implies that board-level governance response (CEO turnover) is both a signal to markets and a functionally effective remediation mechanism.

**Relevance to Hypotheses:**
- **H6 (executive turnover/governance response):** This paper is a direct empirical test of the CEO-dismissal-as-governance-mechanism for H6. The finding that dismissal actually *improves* internal controls (not just signals it) distinguishes functional versus symbolic governance responses — a key distinction for H6's design
- **H3 (prior breach history):** The internal-control deterioration pathway helps explain why second-breach penalties are larger (Peng & Zhang 2023): the first breach may leave latent internal-control weaknesses that are unresolved if the CEO is not replaced
- **H2 (regulation impact):** Disclosure mandates may accelerate CEO replacement by forcing board accountability; this paper provides the second-order chain from regulation → disclosure → governance change → internal-control improvement
- **Overall utility:** Confirm full citation from IRFA 2024; pairs with Liu & Babar (2024) systematic review and Zhang et al. (2025) for H6 coverage

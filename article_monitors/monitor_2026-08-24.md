### Article N68 — NEW ⭐⭐⭐ [2024 FLAG — HIGHLY RELEVANT]
**Akyildirim, E., Conlon, T., Corbet, S. & Hou, Y. (2024).** HACKED: Understanding the stock market response to cyberattacks. *Journal of International Financial Markets, Institutions and Money*, 97, Article 102082.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intfin.2024.102082
**ScienceDirect URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1042443124001483
**ECGI PDF:** https://www.ecgi.global/content/hacked-understanding-stock-market-response-cyberattacks

**Summary:** Akyildirim, Conlon, Corbet, and Hou conduct a large-sample event study of cyberattack disclosures, finding an average stock return of −0.24% on the day after a cyber event. This initial decline reverses in approximately two weeks — distinguishing short-run price pressure from longer-run reputational repricing. The moderation analysis is the paper's central contribution: the magnitude of stock market decrease is greatest for (a) companies with prior cyber event histories (repeat-breach penalty) and (b) breaches deemed most severe by breach-type classification. The paper also identifies a contagion effect — breaches at one firm trigger increased volatility across other firms in the same exchange and even internationally — directly quantifying the sector-level information asymmetry cost.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** ⭐⭐⭐ The heterogeneous market response — concentrated in repeat-breach firms and high-severity events — is the core H1 finding. The two-week return reversal implies that the initial market reaction overestimates the permanent valuation effect, and firm characteristics (breach history, severity classification) determine which firms experience persistent versus transient impacts.
- **H3 (Repeat-breach penalty):** ⭐⭐⭐ The largest negative reactions accrue to firms with prior cyber event histories — the most direct H3 empirical anchor in the 2024 peer-reviewed literature. The severity moderation implies that repeat breaches are not simply additive but compound investor concerns about systemic security culture failures.
- **H4 (Breach type):** ⭐⭐ Severity classification as a moderation variable is the H4 mechanism: "most severe" events (financial data, healthcare records) drive the cross-sectional dispersion in market reactions more than raw breach size.
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** ⭐⭐ The international contagion effect — volatility spillovers across exchanges — extends H5 beyond the breached firm to the global portfolio level. This cross-border volatility transmission is the broadest information asymmetry cost documented in this monitor.
- **H2 (Regulation):** The two-week reversal suggests that even without mandatory disclosure rules, markets eventually price breaches correctly — but the initial -0.24% decline and reversal pattern implies a period of price dislocation consistent with H5's adverse-selection mechanism. Mandatory timely disclosure (FCC rule) should compress this dislocation window.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐⭐ *Journal of International Financial Markets, Institutions and Money (Elsevier) — high-impact peer-reviewed finance journal. 2024 publication, included as highly relevant flag. The repeat-breach penalty finding (H3) and international contagion (H5) are among the strongest empirical anchors in any monitor cycle. Full PDF available via ECGI. Verify whether this was captured in an earlier monitor cycle (N1–N59); if so, treat as a citation update.*

### Article N69 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Muktadir-Al-Mukit, D. & Ali, H.M. (2025).** The dynamics of stock market responses following the cyber-attacks news: evidence from event study. *Information Systems Frontiers*, Springer Nature.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10796-025-10639-6
**Springer URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10639-6
**ResearchGate:** https://www.researchgate.net/publication/396540397

**Summary:** Muktadir-Al-Mukit and Ali conduct a multi-window event study of cyberattack news disclosures, quantifying an average market value loss of $309.33 million per firm on the disclosure date. Cumulative abnormal returns remain significantly negative for up to 250 days post-announcement, documenting the longest post-breach underperformance window found in this monitor cycle. A secondary sector-spillover analysis confirms that a breach in one firm triggers elevated volatility among firms in the same sector and across international exchanges — corroborating the Akyildirim et al. (N68) international contagion finding from a different dataset.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** ⭐⭐ The 250-day persistence of negative CARs is the longest documented information-asymmetry cost arc in this monitor. It implies that breach-induced information asymmetry is not resolved at announcement but persists well into the recovery period — consistent with H5's prediction that voluntary or delayed disclosure extends the adverse-selection window. The sector-spillover volatility finding confirms cross-firm information asymmetry costs (complementing N63, Kelton & Yang).
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** The $309.33M average loss masks firm-level dispersion; the paper's cross-sectional moderator analysis (if available in full text) would directly inform H1's size/sector heterogeneity predictions.
- **H4 (Breach type):** The 250-day CARs suggest that certain breach types — likely financial data and healthcare — drive long-run underperformance; this connects to H4's severity ordering if the full paper provides breach-type breakdowns.
- **H2 (Regulation):** If mandatory FCC disclosure (7-business-day rule) compresses the pre-announcement information asymmetry window, the 250-day post-breach underperformance should be shorter for post-rule disclosures — a testable H2 prediction using Muktadir-Al-Mukit & Ali's methodology with a pre/post rule design.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Information Systems Frontiers (Springer) — peer-reviewed IS journal. 2025. The $309.33M headline figure is a useful benchmarking reference for the dissertation's economic magnitude discussion. The 250-day CAR persistence is the longest documented in this monitor and provides the strongest case for sustained information asymmetry costs (H5).*

### Article N70 — NEW ⭐⭐⭐
**Liu, C. & Babar, M.A. (2026).** Corporate cybersecurity risk and data breaches: A systematic review of empirical research. *Australian Journal of Management*, 51(1), 62–92.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1177/03128962241293658
**SAGE URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03128962241293658
**ResearchGate:** https://www.researchgate.net/publication/388406170

**Summary:** Liu and Babar (University of Adelaide) synthesize 203 empirical studies on corporate cybersecurity risk and data breaches across 12 academic disciplines, producing the most comprehensive systematic review of the literature to date. The review develops a novel framework mapping the inter-relationships between (1) the drivers of cybersecurity risk, (2) the impacts of cyberattacks on firms, and (3) feedback mechanisms enabling firms to learn from breach events. Critically, the review identifies firm-level heterogeneity in breach impact as an understudied dimension and notes that breached firms decrease R&D expenditures with diminished innovation outcomes (fewer patents, lower asset intangibility). The authors explicitly call for future research to explore firm-level heterogeneity in determining the impacts of and responses to cyberattacks.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** ⭐⭐⭐ The systematic review's call for firm-level heterogeneity research is a direct validation of H1's research design. Citing Liu & Babar as establishing the gap that H1 addresses provides a robust literature-gap justification. The 203-study synthesis covers more cross-sectional variation than any primary study, making it the ideal Chapter 2 literature positioning anchor.
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** ⭐⭐ The R&D reduction and innovation decline findings are the long-run economic consequences of breach events — distinct from stock-price effects but indicative of the sustained real costs H5's information asymmetry predicts (investor uncertainty about future productive capacity).
- **All hypotheses:** As a systematic review covering 12 disciplines, Liu & Babar provide a cross-disciplinary justification for each hypothesis. The framework's "feedback mechanism" dimension (how firms learn from prior breaches) is directly relevant to H3's repeat-breach penalty: if learning-from-breach reduces subsequent breach probability, H3's penalty may be partially offset by improved cybersecurity investment in repeat-breach firms.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐⭐ *Australian Journal of Management (SAGE) — peer-reviewed management journal, 2026. This is the most comprehensive systematic review in the monitor and should anchor Chapter 2's literature review. The explicit heterogeneity research call validates H1's contribution. Pairs with prior systematic reviews (if any in earlier monitors) to establish the literature's trajectory. Full PDF available via SAGE Journals (institutional access).*

### Article N71 — NEW ⭐⭐⭐ [SHARP NULL — CRITICAL FOR H2]
**Bangalore, S. (2026).** When public information becomes tradeable: A pre-registered event study of SEC Item 1.05 cyber-incident disclosures, 2023–2026. SSRN Working Paper 6713818. Posted May 4, 2026.
**SSRN URL:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6713818

**Summary:** Bangalore (2026) presents the first pre-registered event study of SEC Item 1.05 Form 8-K cyber-incident disclosures, covering 82 filings (66 Item 1.05 filings) from June 2021 through April 8, 2026. The primary test yields an alpha of −0.03% with a two-way firm-by-month clustered t-statistic of −0.02 — a sharp statistical null: the study cannot reject zero tradeable T+5 abnormal return from mandatory SEC cyber-incident disclosures. The paper explicitly frames this as a test of whether the SEC's 2023 mandatory disclosure rule creates exploitable market information, and finds that the disclosed information is either already priced before the 8-K filing (consistent with pre-announcement leakage, N60) or does not materially update investor valuations. This is the most direct empirical test of the market reaction to mandatory SEC disclosure filings using the post-rule 2023–2026 period.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐⭐ The sharp null at announcement day is the most direct challenge to H2's assumed mechanism: if mandatory 8-K disclosures don't produce tradeable abnormal returns, the regulatory effect on stock prices operates through channels other than day-of-disclosure repricing. Bangalore's null is consistent with the pre-announcement leakage interpretation (N60): information reaches the market before mandatory disclosure, meaning the regulation's value is in compressing the pre-announcement window (reducing prior information asymmetry) rather than producing a day-of-disclosure price impact. This reframes H2 — mandatory disclosure value may be measured in reduced pre-announcement volatility and bid-ask spreads, not in event-study CARs.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** ⭐⭐⭐ If information is already priced at the 8-K filing date, the information asymmetry must have been resolved before filing — implying that H5's adverse-selection costs peak in the pre-disclosure window and are substantially resolved by institutional investors before the mandatory filing. H5's empirical design should focus on pre-announcement trading metrics (bid-ask spreads, short interest, options activity) rather than post-announcement CARs.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** A pre-registered null across the full sample does not preclude cross-sectional H1 heterogeneity: small firms, high-leverage firms, or first-time breach firms may still show significant CARs even if the pooled average is zero. The pre-registration makes Bangalore's cross-sectional moderator analysis (if conducted) particularly credible.
- **Pairing with Hydari et al. (N67):** Bangalore's null (no market reaction to mandatory disclosure) and Hydari et al.'s null (no customer revenue reaction to disclosure laws) together constitute a "dual-null" challenge to both market-based and customer-based deterrence mechanisms — a critical pair for the dissertation's H2 counterargument section.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐⭐ *SSRN working paper (pre-registered), posted May 2026. Pre-registration substantially increases credibility vs. non-pre-registered null results — the null cannot be attributed to publication bias or researcher degrees of freedom. Monitor for journal submission. This is the single most important new paper for H2 found this cycle — the first pre-registered null on the post-rule SEC mandatory disclosure period. Should appear in Chapter 2 alongside N67 as the strongest skeptical evidence on H2's mechanism.*

**⚠️ Note: Author names could not be confirmed from available search results — verify full citation before dissertation use.**

**Summary:** This systematic literature review on the Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) role identifies several key understudied challenges: establishing legitimacy within the C-suite executive team, appropriate accountability structures for cybersecurity incidents, CISO turnover dynamics, and promoting security investment in the face of business realities and budget constraints. The review explicitly identifies CISO turnover as a recurring phenomenon tied to breach accountability and notes that CISOs often serve as scapegoats following major incidents — a dynamic distinct from CEO or CIO turnover (which responds to broader financial performance). The paper establishes a CISO-specific research agenda that H6's executive turnover hypothesis can leverage for the distinction between CEO, CIO, and CISO turnover responses to breach events.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** ⭐⭐⭐ The CISO-specific turnover literature is the most important gap in H6's coverage across all monitors. Prior monitors documented CEO/CIO turnover (N17, prior articles) but the CISO dimension — a C-suite role whose entire mandate is cybersecurity — has been underrepresented. This review provides the theoretical and empirical framing for a CISO-specific H6 prediction: CISO departure rates should be higher than CEO departure rates following major breaches, and this differential should increase post-FCC rule adoption (when CISO accountability is more formally established by SEC governance disclosure requirements).
- **H2 (Regulation):** SEC's 2023 disclosure rule (Item 1C in 10-K) requires annual cybersecurity governance disclosure, including board and management roles in cybersecurity oversight. This regulatory context elevates CISO visibility and accountability — potentially accelerating CISO turnover following disclosed breaches as boards seek to demonstrate governance responsiveness.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** CISO legitimacy within the C-suite varies by firm size and industry: large firms are more likely to have CISO positions with board-level access, while small firms may delegate cybersecurity to IT leadership. This size-based variation in CISO institutional position predicts H1 heterogeneity in governance response to breaches.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Computers & Security (Elsevier) — 2024, peer-reviewed. Author verification required before dissertation citation. This is the strongest H6 anchor for CISO-specific turnover found in any monitor cycle — prior H6 articles focused on CEO/CIO responses. If H6 is expanded to distinguish CISO turnover from C-suite turnover broadly, this paper establishes the justification.*

## KEY NEW INSIGHTS THIS CYCLE

### "Dual-Null" Challenge to H2's Deterrence Mechanism — Now 3 papers:
- **N71 (Bangalore, 2026, pre-registered):** No tradeable T+5 abnormal return from mandatory SEC 8-K Item 1.05 disclosures
- **N67 (Hydari, Liang & Telang, 2024):** No customer revenue punishment from data breach disclosure laws
- **N60 (Viancourt & Walkup, 2026):** Pre-announcement data leakage implies information reaches markets before mandatory filing

**New synthesis:** The three null/leakage results together suggest that mandatory disclosure rules' primary value is not in the day-of-disclosure price impact (N71 null) or customer deterrence (N67 null), but in compressing the pre-announcement adverse-selection window (the period when insiders but not investors know about a breach — N60's leakage evidence). H2's empirical design should therefore measure regulation's impact on pre-announcement bid-ask spreads, short interest spikes, and volatility (H5 metrics) rather than relying solely on day-of-disclosure CARs.

### International Contagion Cluster (H5) — Now 3 papers:
- **N68 (Akyildirim et al., 2024):** Breach at one firm → increased volatility across same-exchange and cross-border firms
- **N69 (Muktadir-Al-Mukit & Ali, 2025):** 250-day CAR persistence; sector-wide volatility spillovers
- **N63 (Kelton & Yang, 2025):** Bystander firm contagion susceptibility reduced by strong internal controls

**New synthesis:** H5's information asymmetry cost is demonstrably not confined to the breached firm or even to the domestic market — it extends internationally (N68) and across industry peers (N63, N69). This global spillover dimension strengthens the case for mandatory timely disclosure: faster disclosure reduces the window during which international portfolio investors must price unresolved cyber risk.

### H3 (Repeat-Breach Penalty) Confirmation — 2024 Peer-Reviewed Addition:
- **N68 (Akyildirim et al., 2024):** The magnitude of negative stock market reaction is greatest for companies with prior breach histories — peer-reviewed, published in a top finance journal.

This is the strongest H3 empirical anchor from a peer-reviewed 2024 source. Prior H3 anchors in the monitor were from working papers or earlier years; N68 fills the peer-reviewed confirmation gap.

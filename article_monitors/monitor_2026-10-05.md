### 1. Liu, C. & Babar, M. A. (2026)

**Title:** Corporate cybersecurity risk and data breaches: A systematic review of empirical research

**Journal:** *Australian Journal of Management*, Vol. 51, No. 1, pp. 62–92

**DOI:** [10.1177/03128962241293658](https://doi.org/10.1177/03128962241293658)

**Summary:** A systematic review of 203 empirical studies across 12 disciplines mapping drivers of cybersecurity risk, consequences of cyberattacks, and feedback mechanisms. The review synthesises the literature on how firm characteristics and regulatory context moderate breach market impacts. It identifies critical gaps in research on cross-country regulatory variation and long-run governance responses.

**Dissertation Relevance:**
- **H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, H6** — Provides a comprehensive empirical map that grounds all six hypotheses; particularly useful for framing the theoretical contribution and identifying under-studied areas (firm heterogeneity, disclosure timing, regulatory heterogeneity).

### 2. Foerderer, J. & Schuetz, S. W. (2022)

**Title:** Data Breach Announcements and Stock Market Reactions: A Matter of Timing?

**Journal:** *Management Science*, Vol. 68, No. 10, pp. 7298–7322

**DOI:** [10.1287/mnsc.2021.4264](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2021.4264)

**Summary:** Using event study methods on a large breach sample, the authors find that negative abnormal returns from breaches are attenuated when disclosed on high-news-pressure days (median CAR falls from −$347M to −$85M at +1 SD news pressure). Firms strategically time announcements to coincide with busy news cycles — effect is strongest for severe, internally caused breaches leaking health or identity data.

**Dissertation Relevance:**
- **H1** — Core evidence that *timing* is an active strategic lever distinct from firm characteristics; critical counterpoint to pure event-window CAR literature.
- **H4** — Documents breach-type-specific timing strategies (health data, credential breaches disclosed on busiest days).

### 3. Obaydin, I., Zurbruegg, R., Mihaylov, G. & Truong, C. (2024)

**Title:** The unintended cost of data breach notification laws: Evidence from managerial bad news hoarding

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Finance & Accounting*, March 2024

**DOI:** [10.1111/jbfa.12794](https://doi.org/10.1111/jbfa.12794)

**Summary:** Exploiting staggered state-level adoption of data breach notification (DBN) laws as a natural experiment, this paper documents that DBN laws significantly increase stock price crash risk. The mechanism is managerial bad-news hoarding: facing the obligation to disclose breach events, managers accumulate other negative news to moderate perceived overreaction. The effect is stronger for managers with greater incentive or ability to suppress information.

**Dissertation Relevance:**
- **H2** — Directly estimates causal regulatory impact (DBN laws) on market outcomes via quasi-experimental design — template for H2's FCC rule analysis.
- **H5** — Crash risk is a direct proxy for information asymmetry accumulation; connects regulation to H5's volatility hypothesis.

### 4. Cao, H., Phan, H. V. & Silveri, S. (2024)

**Title:** Data breach disclosures and stock price crash risk: Evidence from data breach notification laws

**Journal:** *International Review of Financial Analysis*, Vol. 93, Article 103164

**DOI:** [10.1016/j.irfa.2024.103164](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.irfa.2024.103164)

**Summary:** Uses staggered DBN law adoption as an exogenous shock and finds mandatory disclosure significantly raises future crash risk. The effect is strongest for firms with weaker corporate governance, higher financial constraints, and higher ex-ante information asymmetry. Investor anxiety about data security post-disclosure drives the elevated crash probability.

**Dissertation Relevance:**
- **H2** — Parallel to Obaydin et al. (2024) but from a different methodological angle; confirms regulatory mandate → crash risk channel.
- **H5** — Directly links information asymmetry (bid-ask spread, analyst dispersion proxies) to crash risk post-regulation; provides empirical scaffolding for H5.

### 5. Peng, J., Zhang, H., Mao, J. & Xu, S. (2023)

**Title:** Repeated data breaches and firm value

**Journal:** *Economics Letters*, Vol. 224, Article 111001

**DOI:** [10.1016/j.econlet.2023.111001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econlet.2023.111001)

**Summary:** First study focused on sequential breach effects; finds second breaches impose larger valuation losses than first breaches, with financial-data breaches generating the greatest damage. Critically, CEO replacement after the first breach prevents future losses, while third and subsequent breaches have no incremental impact — suggesting market expectations converge after repeated incidents.

**Dissertation Relevance:**
- **H3** — Direct empirical support for a prior-breach penalty in market reactions; offers nuance on the dose-response curve (largest at second breach, plateau thereafter).
- **H4** — Financial-data breaches identified as the most value-destructive breach type.
- **H6** — CEO replacement post-first-breach eliminates subsequent valuation loss — key evidence for governance mechanism.

### 6. Akyildirim, E., Conlon, T., Corbet, S. & Hou, Y. (2024)

**Title:** HACKED: Understanding the stock market response to cyberattacks

**Journal:** *Journal of International Financial Markets, Institutions and Money*, Vol. 97, Article 102082

**DOI:** [10.1016/j.intfin.2024.102082](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intfin.2024.102082)

**Summary:** International sample of cyberattacks and privacy violations; finds −0.24% stock return on the day following a cyber event, reversing within ~two weeks. First-time incidents generate significantly more severe reactions than repeat events. Market decline severity is related to firm-level characteristics: size, volatility, credit ranking, and asset volatility.

**Dissertation Relevance:**
- **H1** — Confirms firm-size and volatility characteristics moderate price reactions (complements timing dimension from Foerderer & Schuetz 2022).
- **H3** — Cross-validates the repeat-breach attenuation finding of Peng et al. (2023) in international context.
- **H5** — Asset volatility moderates severity, linking pre-breach firm risk profile to post-breach market response.

### 7. Rasoulian, S., Grégoire, Y., Legoux, R. & Sénécal, S. (2023)

**Title:** The Effects of Service Crises and Recovery Resources on Market Reactions: An Event Study Analysis on Data Breach Announcements

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Research*, Vol. 26

**DOI:** [10.1177/10946705211036944](https://doi.org/10.1177/10946705211036944)

**Summary:** Event study on 217 breach announcements; demonstrates that negative abnormal returns increase when breach outcome (e.g., financial data leaked) or causal process (hacker attack) signals high severity. Firm recovery resources — age, size, profitability, liquidity, brand familiarity — cushion returns particularly in financial-data breach scenarios. Small, young, less-profitable firms exhibit the worst post-breach performance.

**Dissertation Relevance:**
- **H1** — Recovery resources (proxies for firm characteristics) moderate market reaction — directly tests firm heterogeneity thesis.
- **H4** — Breach seriousness operationalized via outcome type (financial data) and cause (external hacker vs. internal error); distinguishes breach type effects cleanly.
- **H5** (implied) — Liquidity as a recovery resource modulates volatility channel post-breach.

### 8. Celeny, D. & Maréchal, L. (2024)

**Title:** Cyber risk and the cross-section of stock returns

**Source:** Working paper / arXiv preprint

**DOI/arXiv:** [10.48550/arXiv.2402.04775](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2402.04775) | [SSRN 4587993](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4587993)

**Summary:** Constructs a firm-level cyber risk score using machine learning text analysis of SEC disclosures. Finds high-cyber-risk stocks earn 18.72% p.a. excess returns, and a long-short cyber risk factor generates a significant 6.93% p.a. risk premium robust to all standard factor benchmarks. Using Bayesian asset pricing, cyber risk is the single feature needed to price the cross-section.

**Dissertation Relevance:**
- **H5** — Strong evidence that cyber risk (pre-breach) is priced in equilibrium as an information asymmetry premium, supporting H5's volatility/asymmetry hypothesis.
- **H1** — Firm-level cyber risk scores vary systematically across characteristics, providing a tool for testing firm heterogeneity in breach impacts.

### 9. Oyima-Antseleve, N. (2026)

**Title:** Expectations and market reactions to data breaches

**Journal:** *Finance Research Letters*, Vol. 90, Article 109201

**DOI:** [10.1016/j.frl.2025.109201](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.frl.2025.109201)

**Summary:** Demonstrates that investor reactions are driven by whether breach severity exceeds *ex-ante expectations*, not absolute breach size. Unexpectedly severe breaches produce significant negative CARs that predict subsequent sales declines, confirming investor ability to anticipate downstream reputational damage. Financial-data breaches amplify the reaction to unexpected severity.

**Dissertation Relevance:**
- **H1** — Investor expectations (a function of firm disclosure transparency and prior breach history) mediate price impact — nuances the timing vs. firm-characteristics question.
- **H3** — Expectation calibration to prior breach history is a key mechanism: repeat-breach firms see expectations updated, reducing incremental CAR.
- **H4** — Financial-data breach type amplifies the expectations-gap effect.

### 10. Andrew, J. et al. (2026)

**Title:** Exploring the Materiality of Data Breach Disclosures on the Australian Stock Exchange

**Journal:** *Abacus*, Vol. 62, No. 3, pp. 673–697

**DOI:** [10.1111/abac.70001](https://doi.org/10.1111/abac.70001)

**Summary:** Examines ASX-listed company breach disclosures and finds a systematic lack of disclosure and notable absence of detail where disclosures do occur. The concept of "materiality" is examined through a detailed case study of Landmark White (LMW). Investor-stakeholder relationships are damaged most where management applies an unduly high materiality threshold before disclosing.

**Dissertation Relevance:**
- **H2** — Comparative regulatory angle: ASX continuous-disclosure regime vs. U.S. FCC/SEC mandate creates natural variation for evaluating whether specific disclosure rules change outcomes.
- **H5** — Opacity/non-disclosure creates information asymmetry precisely the regime FCC rules are intended to resolve.

### 11. Obaydin, I., Zurbruegg, R., Mihaylov, G. & Truong, C. (2026 — Working Paper)

**Title:** Mandatory data breach disclosure laws and stock liquidity: The role of cybersecurity risk and reporting opacity

**Source:** SSRN Working Paper

**DOI/SSRN:** [10.2139/ssrn.7233704](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7233704)

**Summary:** Stacked DiD exploiting staggered DBN law adoption finds that mandatory disclosure reduces bid-ask spreads by ~7.5–8.3%. However, net liquidity benefit accrues only to low-cyber-risk firms; high-cyber-risk firms see disclosure costs and litigation risk offset the gain. Within the low-risk group, benefits are concentrated in less opaque (more transparent) firms — disclosure mandates and reporting quality are complements.

**Dissertation Relevance:**
- **H2** — Quantifies the stock liquidity channel of disclosure regulation; the heterogeneous treatment effect across cyber-risk levels directly informs H2's firm-size heterogeneity dimension.
- **H5** — Bid-ask spread reduction as the liquidity/information asymmetry outcome directly tests H5.
- **H1** — Heterogeneity in regulatory impact across firm cyber-risk profiles connects to firm-characteristics moderation.

## Flagged — Highly Relevant from 2015–2020

### 12. ⭐ Say, G. & Vasudeva, G. (2020)

**Title:** Learning from Digital Failures? The Effectiveness of Firms' Divestiture and Management Turnover Responses to Data Breaches

**Journal:** *Strategy Science (INFORMS)*, Vol. 5, No. 2, pp. 117–142

**DOI:** [10.1287/stsc.2020.0106](https://doi.org/10.1287/stsc.2020.0106)

**Summary:** Panel study of 8,760 publicly traded U.S. firms (2005–2016). Data breaches significantly increase likelihood of unrelated divestitures and CTO turnover. Unrelated divestitures reduce future breach reoccurrence; CTO turnover alone has no significant preventive effect. The governance response literature's key finding: structural restructuring (divestiture) rather than personnel substitution drives improvement.

**Dissertation Relevance:**
- **H6** — Foundational study for executive turnover thesis; distinguishes which governance responses matter (divestiture > CTO replacement) and which do not.
- **H1** — Firm-level governance characteristics pre-breach moderate likelihood of remediation response.

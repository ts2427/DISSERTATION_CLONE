### Article N40 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Oyima-Antseleve, N. (2025).** Expectations and market reactions to data breaches. *Finance Research Letters*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.frl.2025.109201
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S154461232502450X
**SSRN preprint:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5240536
**Published:** Finance Research Letters (Elsevier), 2025; posted SSRN May 2025

**Summary:** Using breach data from 2005 to 2019, this paper proposes that cross-sectional variation in stock price reactions to breach announcements is driven primarily by whether breach severity *exceeds* investors' ex-ante expectations — not by absolute breach size. Breaches that surprise investors with unexpectedly severe outcomes generate significantly larger negative abnormal returns, and these market reactions are forward-looking: they accurately predict subsequent declines in firm sales, suggesting investors correctly price the reputational and customer-loyalty consequences of severe breaches. The paper also finds that financial data breaches amplify market reactions to unexpected severity, demonstrating that data sensitivity materially shapes investor assessment.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** ⭐⭐ The expectation-formation framework directly extends H1: investor expectations are shaped by observable firm characteristics (prior breach history, sector, data sensitivity), so unexpected breach severity generates heterogeneous reactions across the cross-section. The finding that financial data amplifies reactions supports H1's prediction of differential impacts by firm type and data exposure.
- **H4 (Breach type):** Financial data breaches amplify reactions to severity surprises — a specific H4 result linking breach type to the expectation-deviation mechanism. Pairs with H4's taxonomy of breach-type market effects.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** Unexpected severity implies higher ex-post information asymmetry between firm managers and investors — the expectation-gap is a direct H5 channel. Mandatory disclosure (H2) could reduce expectation errors by standardizing disclosure content.
- **H2 (Regulation → market outcomes):** If mandatory disclosure rules improve information, they should reduce ex-ante expectation errors, compressing the abnormal-return distribution at announcement. This paper provides the pre-regulation baseline for such a before/after regulatory study.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Expectation-based framework is methodologically novel.* This paper directly addresses why some breaches generate large market reactions and others do not — the H1 heterogeneity puzzle. Cite alongside Management Science (2021) "A Matter of Timing" paper and Gordon et al. (N33) as the trio explaining return variation in breach event studies.

### Article N41 — NEW ⭐
**Wu, H., Ma, M. & Zhang, J. (2025).** Impact of cybersecurity disclosure frequency on stock price crash risk. *Journal of Corporate Accounting & Finance*, *36*, 87–102.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1002/jcaf.22775
**URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jcaf.22775
**Published:** Journal of Corporate Accounting & Finance (Wiley), Vol. 36, 2025

**Summary:** Using textual analysis of cybersecurity disclosures in financial reports of Chinese A-share public companies from 2017–2022, the paper finds that *higher disclosure frequency* is associated with *greater* future stock price crash risk — a counterintuitive result consistent with information-overload and investor-sentiment deterioration. The effect is most pronounced for firms audited by non-Big-4 auditors and those with lower analyst coverage (higher pre-existing information asymmetry). The mechanism is dual: increased disclosures amplify investor anxiety (sentiment channel) while also signaling ongoing cyber vulnerabilities (information asymmetry channel). The paper connects cybersecurity disclosure policy to the stock price crash risk literature pioneered by Jin and Myers (2006).

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** The crash-risk amplification from high disclosure frequency is a novel H5 result — more disclosure does not always reduce asymmetry. The interaction with analyst coverage (a standard H5 proxy) confirms that the mechanism runs through information asymmetry rather than institutional quality alone.
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** Mandatory disclosure requirements (FCC/SEC) that increase disclosure frequency for previously non-disclosing firms may paradoxically increase crash risk — consistent with Obaydin et al. (N19) and Cao et al. (N27). Adds a frequency dimension to H2's regulation-impact analysis.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Non-Big-4 auditor and low analyst coverage are firm-characteristic moderators of the frequency-crash relationship, consistent with H1's prediction that weaker-governance firms face amplified regulatory impacts.
- **NOTE:** Sample is Chinese A-shares — institutional context differs from U.S. FCC/SEC framework. Flag as international comparison evidence and pair with U.S.-sample results for H2's robustness discussion. The 2017–2022 period covers the early-stage mandatory cybersecurity disclosure regime in China.

### Article N42 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Authors TBC — verify via ScienceDirect pii: S0167404825004419 (2025).** Reassessing information security perceptions following a data breach announcement: The role of post-breach management in firm-specific risk. *Computers & Security*.
**DOI:** TBC — confirm via ScienceDirect pii: S0167404825004419
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167404825004419
**Published:** Computers & Security (Elsevier), November 2025

**Summary:** Applying legitimacy theory to a sample of 485 U.S. data breach announcements, the paper examines how investor perceptions of a firm's information security practices — termed "information security legitimacy" — shape long-run firm-specific risk following a breach disclosure. The central finding is that firms with stronger information security legitimacy experience significantly lower firm-specific risk (measured over a six-month post-announcement window), and that shorter delays between the breach event and public announcement substantially strengthen this risk-reduction effect. The study offers a unified framework connecting post-breach management actions (timely disclosure, proactive security communications) to long-term financial risk outcomes through the stakeholder-legitimacy channel.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** ⭐⭐ Information security legitimacy is a firm-characteristic governance quality variable directly consistent with H1's prediction that firms with stronger security postures experience attenuated market penalties. The 485-firm sample provides sufficient cross-sectional variation to test H1-style moderation hypotheses.
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** Mandatory disclosure requirements that reduce announcement delays (the FCC's 72-hour and SEC's 4-day rules) should directly activate the risk-reduction mechanism documented here — providing H2 with an empirically-grounded mechanism through which mandatory disclosure timing rules affect long-run stock risk.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** The six-month firm-specific risk reduction is consistent with post-announcement information asymmetry resolution under H5 — firms with high legitimacy and rapid disclosure accelerate price discovery and reduce prolonged volatility.
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** Post-breach management response (timely disclosure, communications) is a governance-quality signal that connects to board-level accountability — firms that handle breaches well may face less executive turnover pressure (a moderator of H6).
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *First large-sample legitimacy-theory application to post-breach management and long-run risk.* The 485-firm sample and six-month event window extend H5 and H1 evidence beyond the typical 3–10 day event study. Authors TBC — confirm via publisher page before dissertation citation.

### Article N43 — NEW ⭐
**Authors TBC — verify via ScienceDirect pii: S1467089525000053 (2025).** Impact of reporting information security breaches, accounting quality, and the opportunistic disclosure of good news and bad news. *International Journal of Accounting Information Systems*, *56*.
**DOI:** TBC — confirm via ScienceDirect pii: S1467089525000053
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1467089525000053
**Published:** International Journal of Accounting Information Systems (Elsevier), Vol. 56, January 2025

**Summary:** Examines the intersection of information security breach (ISB) reporting, accounting quality, and strategic disclosure timing. Key findings: (1) firms that report ISBs face significantly higher stock price crash risk; (2) breached firms' release of good news is *negatively* associated with crash risk — consistent with managers using positive news to cushion breach announcement impacts; (3) bad news disclosure by breached firms is *positively* associated with crash risk, suggesting that when managers finally release accumulated negative information, crash risk spikes. The interplay of ISB reporting with opportunistic disclosure timing reveals that managers of breached firms actively manage the market's news environment, introducing a new mechanism by which breaches affect return distributions beyond the announcement-day event.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** Mandatory disclosure rules that constrain opportunistic good-news timing (by requiring prompt breach disclosure regardless of concurrent news environment) would disrupt the cushioning mechanism documented here — a novel regulatory-design implication for H2.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** The strategic timing of news around breach disclosures is a form of managerial information management that sustains information asymmetry between managers and investors — directly extending H5's information-asymmetry channel to include managerial strategic disclosure behavior.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Accounting quality (accruals, earnings management) interacts with breach reporting to determine crash risk — accounting-quality variation is a firm-characteristic moderator consistent with H1's heterogeneity framework.
- **NOTE:** Authors TBC — confirm via publisher page before dissertation submission. Pairs naturally with N19 (Obaydin et al., 2024) on bad-news hoarding, N27 (Cao et al., 2024) on crash risk from DBN laws, and N41 (Wu et al., 2025) on disclosure frequency and crash risk — together these four papers constitute a "crash risk" cluster for H2 and H5.

### Article N44 — NEW ⭐
**López, F.A., Jara-Sarrúa, L., Morales-Parada, F. & Palos-Sánchez, P.R. (2025).** Cybersecurity disclosure in the financial sector: An examination of the influence of incident exposure, governance practices, and regulatory context. *Electronic Commerce Research*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10660-025-10081-5
**URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10660-025-10081-5
**Published:** Electronic Commerce Research (Springer), 2025

**Summary:** Using a custom cybersecurity disclosure index derived from content analysis of banks' annual reports, the paper examines how three drivers shape the volume and quality of cybersecurity information disclosed by financial-sector firms: (1) incident exposure (prior breach experience), (2) governance practices (board structure, audit committee), and (3) regulatory context (jurisdiction and applicable rules). Anchored in legitimacy theory, the study finds that regulatory pressure is the dominant driver of disclosure volume and standardization, while governance practices shape disclosure quality — and incident exposure produces a paradoxically *negative* disclosure effect in some specifications, consistent with firms reducing disclosure after breaches to limit litigation exposure.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** Regulatory context is the primary disclosure driver — directly relevant to H2's prediction that the FCC's 2024 mandatory rule shifts disclosure behavior for telecom carriers. The banking sector analogy (where prudential regulators similarly mandate cyber disclosures) provides a regulatory-design comparison case.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Governance practices (board structure) shape disclosure quality, with governance-quality heterogeneity producing cross-sectional differences in disclosure content — consistent with H1's firm-characteristic moderation.
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** The negative post-breach disclosure effect (firms reduce disclosure after incidents to limit litigation) is a governance mechanism that may also suppress H3's repeat-breach market penalty — firms that reduce transparency after first breaches face compounding litigation-vs-transparency tradeoffs in repeat events.
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** Board-level governance practices are antecedents to both disclosure behavior and post-breach executive accountability — connecting this paper to H6's governance-quality channel.
- **NOTE:** Financial sector (banking) sample; FCC context is telecommunications. The regulatory-driver finding is directly analogous to the FCC 2024 telecom mandatory-disclosure rule but must be qualified for sector differences. Confirm full author list via Springer link before final citation.

### Article N45 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Agarwal, N., Chatterjee, C. & Agarwal, S. (2024).** Data breach notification laws and the cost of private debt. *The British Accounting Review*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bar.2024.101518
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0890838924002981
**Published:** The British Accounting Review (Elsevier), November 2024

**Summary:** Exploiting the staggered U.S. state-level adoption of data breach notification (DBN) laws, this paper documents that mandatory disclosure legislation significantly *increases* firms' cost of private debt — lenders reprice credit risk upward when disclosure laws force firms to reveal cybersecurity vulnerabilities. The increase is most pronounced for (1) industries with high breach susceptibility, (2) firms reporting internal control weaknesses, and (3) firms that have already disclosed cybersecurity risks in their annual reports. Crucially, firms that invest in cybersecurity infrastructure or appoint technology-specialist board members experience a significantly smaller cost-of-debt increase — demonstrating that governance and investment quality can partially offset the regulatory burden. The quasi-experimental design provides credible causal identification via difference-in-differences.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns / market outcomes):** ⭐⭐ Extends the regulatory-impact literature from equity markets to debt markets — mandatory disclosure laws affect the full capital structure cost, not just stock returns. The finding that cybersecurity investment and board technology expertise mitigate the debt cost increase provides H2 with a heterogeneous-firm-response mechanism: firms with strong cyber governance face lower regulatory cost burdens, which should translate to attenuated stock-return penalties as well.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Internal control quality, cybersecurity risk disclosure history, and board technology expertise are H1-consistent moderators of the regulatory impact. Industry breach susceptibility provides sector-level H1 variation.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** Lender repricing reflects elevated credit information asymmetry — DBN laws force disclosure that reveals previously hidden vulnerability levels, causing lenders to reprice the (now-revealed) risk premium.
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** Firms in high-susceptibility industries face compounding cost-of-debt increases under DBN laws — a debt-market parallel to H3's equity-market repeat-breach penalty.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Quasi-experimental causal design with clear debt-market extension.* The University of Western Australia / University of Texas team provides a novel capital-structure channel for H2's regulatory-impact findings. Directly complements Obaydin et al. (N19, equity-crash-risk) and Cao et al. (N27, crash risk) — together these three papers cover stock crash risk, equity abnormal returns, and debt pricing as the three capital-market consequences of mandatory disclosure regulation.

### Article N46 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Amani, F., Magnan, M. & Moldovan, R. (2025).** Cybersecurity risks and incidents disclosure: A literature review. *Accounting Perspectives*, *24*(3), 605–667.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/1911-3838.12411
**URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1911-3838.12411
**Published:** Accounting Perspectives (Wiley), Vol. 24, Issue 3, 2025

**Summary:** A comprehensive structured literature review of empirical and analytical research on cybersecurity risk and incident disclosure, covering studies through end-2024. The authors classify the literature along three dimensions: (1) *characteristics* of cybersecurity disclosure (what firms disclose and how); (2) *determinants* of disclosure (firm-level, industry, regulatory); and (3) *outcomes* of disclosure (market reactions, information asymmetry, cost of capital). The review identifies significant gaps in understanding how regulatory mandates (particularly the SEC's 2023 rule and GDPR) translate into measurable market outcomes and documents that most empirical research focuses on post-breach market reactions rather than the disclosure process itself. The accounting-lens framing (Concordia University and McGill University authors) distinguishes this from Liu & Babar (N24, management/IS focus) and Cao et al. systematic reviews.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ The review explicitly documents the gap in understanding how disclosure mandates affect capital-market outcomes — this is precisely the gap the dissertation fills for the FCC 2024 context. Citing this review as the gap-justification anchor for H2 is strong rhetorical and methodological positioning.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** The outcomes dimension synthesizes the information-asymmetry literature — bid-ask spreads, trading volume, analyst forecast dispersion — as post-disclosure market outcomes. Provides the theoretical map for H5's measurement choices.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** The determinants dimension maps which firm-level factors (size, governance, sector) drive disclosure quality and quantity — the H1 cross-sectional framework's empirical foundation.
- **H4 (Breach type):** Characteristics dimension covers breach-type taxonomies and their disclosure patterns — a useful H4 reference for breach classification methodology.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Second comprehensive literature review added this cycle (complementing Liu & Babar, N24).* Accounting Perspectives is an ABDC A-ranked journal; the Amani, Magnan & Moldovan team from Concordia/McGill brings accounting-quality expertise. Use N24 for market-impact evidence synthesis and N46 for disclosure-process gap identification. Both should appear prominently in the dissertation's Chapter 2 gap analysis.

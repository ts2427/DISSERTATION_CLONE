### Article N99 — NEW ⭐⭐⭐ [2025 WORKING PAPER]
**Block, M. (2025).** Market Reactions to Material Cybersecurity Incident Disclosures. *arXiv*, 2512.06144.
**arXiv URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06144
**PDF:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06144
**Submitted:** December 5, 2025 (arXiv q-fin.MF)

**Summary:** Maxwell Block examines short-term stock market reactions to material cybersecurity incidents disclosed under Item 1.05 of Form 8-K, using a sample of disclosures made between 2023 and 2025 — the first two years of mandatory SEC disclosure. The study finds that companies, on average, experience negative price reactions following mandatory disclosure of a material cybersecurity incident. This is among the first academic working papers to directly measure the market effect of the new SEC 8-K Item 1.05 regime, providing an empirical baseline for the post-mandate era.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (SEC mandatory disclosure → stock returns):** ⭐⭐⭐ This is the most directly relevant H2 paper found this cycle. Block's study covers the exact post-SEC-rule window (2023–2025) and measures negative price reactions to mandatory disclosures — directly testing H2's prediction that the mandatory regime affects market pricing. The negative-return finding confirms that markets do react to mandatory disclosures, even though the 10-K evidence (N76) found minimal analyst/investor response to annual risk disclosures. The 8-K incident disclosures appear to carry more market-moving information than forward-looking risk disclosures.
- **H1 (Disclosure timing / firm characteristics):** The event window design captures the timing of disclosure as the key variable — consistent with H1's prediction that disclosure speed moderates market reaction magnitude under mandatory rules.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** Negative price reactions under mandatory disclosure confirm H5's information-release mechanism: forced disclosure immediately resolves uncertainty (and confirms harm), driving negative abnormal returns.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐⭐ *arXiv (q-fin.MF) working paper. Submitted December 5, 2025. Author: Maxwell Block. Not peer-reviewed. This is the first paper found in any monitor cycle that directly studies SEC 8-K Item 1.05 market reactions as an academic event study. Essential for H2. Cite as arXiv working paper; check for a forthcoming published version. DOI: arXiv:2512.06144.*

### Article N100 — NEW ⭐⭐⭐ [2024 PUBLICATION — FLAGGED AS HIGHLY RELEVANT]
**Obaydin, I. (2024).** The unintended cost of data breach notification laws: Evidence from managerial bad news hoarding. *Journal of Business Finance & Accounting*, 51(9–10).
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/jbfa.12794
**Wiley URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jbfa.12794
**Published:** 2024 (Journal of Business Finance & Accounting, Wiley — peer-reviewed)
**⚠️ Note:** Published 2024 — flagged here as highly relevant to multiple hypotheses.

**Summary:** Obaydin documents an *unintended* consequence of data breach notification (DBN) laws: rather than increasing disclosure transparency, mandatory notification laws incentivize managers to hoard bad news by delaying or suppressing interim disclosures of negative developments that are not explicitly covered by breach-specific mandates. Exploiting the staggered adoption of state DBN laws, the paper shows that firms subject to notification mandates exhibit higher bad-news hoarding scores and subsequent stock price crash risk. The perverse effect is strongest for firms with higher information asymmetry and weaker corporate governance.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H5 (Information asymmetry / crash risk):** ⭐⭐⭐ The bad-news hoarding mechanism is a direct H5 information asymmetry channel: mandatory disclosure creates a false sense of security that actually increases overall information asymmetry in the non-breach dimension, amplifying eventual crash risk when withheld bad news surfaces. This is the strongest H5 theoretical contribution found this cycle.
- **H2 (Regulation effects):** ⭐⭐⭐ The "perverse incentive" finding fundamentally complicates H2's prediction — mandatory disclosure rules may improve cybersecurity-specific transparency while simultaneously worsening general information asymmetry through managerial substitution effects (disclosing what is mandated, suppressing what is not). H2's empirical design should control for this substitution channel.
- **H3 (Prior breach history):** Firms that have already experienced a breach and are therefore under heightened regulatory scrutiny may shift from hoarding breach-related news to more aggressive hoarding of other categories of bad news, generating a compound H3 effect over time.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** The heterogeneous effect by governance and information asymmetry level directly operationalizes H1's cross-sectional moderators: firms with weaker governance and higher asymmetry face the strongest perverse-incentive effect.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐⭐ *Journal of Business Finance & Accounting (Wiley) — top-tier peer-reviewed accounting/finance journal. Published 2024. Author: Ivan Obaydin. DOI confirmed (10.1111/jbfa.12794). This is one of the most theoretically important papers for H2 and H5 in the literature — the "perverse incentive" finding directly challenges the naive prediction that mandatory disclosure uniformly reduces information asymmetry. No prior monitor cycle identified this article. Essential reading for H2's regulatory framework section and H5's information asymmetry costs discussion.*

- **H2 (Regulation — SEC 8-K design):** The disclosure chain finding reveals an unintended consequence of the SEC's 4-business-day rule: firms may file a minimal initial disclosure within the deadline and supplement with additional material information through amendment filings, potentially frustrating the rule's information-revelation purpose. Relevant for H2's discussion of regulatory design effects.

## KEY NEW INSIGHTS THIS CYCLE

### The "Perverse Incentive" Finding — H2 Critical Complication (N100)

Obaydin (2024) documents that data breach notification laws, while increasing breach-specific disclosure, simultaneously incentivize managers to *increase* bad-news hoarding in other categories, elevating stock price crash risk. This finding has major implications for H2:

- **H2 mechanism complication:** H2's prediction that mandatory FCC/SEC disclosure reduces information asymmetry costs may be partially offset by the perverse-incentive substitution effect — managers comply with cybersecurity mandates while hoarding other bad news. H2's empirical design should use an overall information asymmetry measure (e.g., bid-ask spreads across all information categories, not just cybersecurity-specific) to capture the net effect.
- **H5 theoretical extension:** The crash risk channel in H5 can now be theorized as having two components: (a) the direct breach announcement effect (acute negative abnormal returns), and (b) the cumulative bad-news hoarding crash risk (tail risk from suppressed non-breach information).

### H6 Literature Clarification — Systematic Review (N101)

Liu & Babar (2026) synthesize the conflicting executive turnover findings. The key moderators that may explain the mixed evidence:
- Breach visibility: Publicly prominent breaches with media coverage generate turnover; low-profile breaches do not.
- Firm governance quality: Strong boards are more likely to act on breach events, while weak governance firms show no turnover response.
- Breach severity: Only severe breaches (large-scale, high-sensitivity data, prolonged dwell time) trigger turnover responses.
- Executive role: CIO/CISO turnover is more consistently elevated than CEO turnover.
H6's empirical design should include these moderators to replicate the heterogeneous literature findings.

### SEC Mandatory Disclosure Market Reactions Confirmed (N99)

Block (2025) confirms negative price reactions to SEC 8-K Item 1.05 mandatory disclosures — directly validating H2's prediction in the post-rule window. This contrasts with N76's finding that 10-K annual risk disclosures (Item 1C) generate minimal investor response. The combined picture: mandatory disclosure of *actual incidents* (8-K) moves markets, while mandatory disclosure of *risk management practices* (10-K) does not. H2 should focus empirical tests on incident-level (8-K) disclosures rather than annual risk disclosures.

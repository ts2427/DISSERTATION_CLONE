### Article N54 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Gao, L., Jiang, D., Wang, J., & Wu, Y.W. (2026).** Cybersecurity disclosure mandates and cross-domain compliance spillovers. *SSRN Working Paper*, Abstract ID: 6985858.
**SSRN URL:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6985858
**Posted:** June 23, 2026 (no journal DOI yet — working paper)

**Summary:** Exploiting the staggered adoption of U.S. state-level breach notification laws (BNLs) as a quasi-natural experiment, Gao, Jiang, Wang, and Wu document that mandatory cybersecurity disclosure mandates generate compliance spillovers *beyond* the cyber regulatory domain — specifically, firms subject to BNLs reduce their federal regulatory violations in non-cyber domains by more than 5% in the first year after adoption. The effect is transitory (dissipating after a few years) but extends across multiple major regulatory domains (environmental, labor, financial). Cross-sectional heterogeneity shows the reduction is strongest for firms with greater organizational capacity and more effective internal controls, suggesting the compliance shock mechanism is capacity-contingent. The interpretation is that BNL adoption functions as a governance shock that reforms internal compliance culture more broadly, not merely a narrow cyber-specific requirement.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ Demonstrates that mandatory disclosure mandates produce organizational-level governance improvements beyond the targeted domain — a mechanism directly relevant to H2's prediction that FCC mandatory disclosure will improve firms' overall risk management (and thus long-run stock performance). The spillover finding predicts that post-FCC-rule telecom carriers will show governance improvements in non-cyber dimensions, a testable H2 implication. Pairs with Haapamäki & Sihvonen (N48) on mandatory-regime compliance effects.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** The capacity-contingent heterogeneity finding shows that smaller or less governance-mature firms capture fewer compliance benefits from mandatory disclosure — a H1 cross-sectional moderator of regulatory effectiveness. Firms with weaker internal controls (a firm-characteristic dimension) benefit less from the mandate.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** Cross-domain regulatory compliance reduction → improved information quality → lower information asymmetry costs, consistent with H5's prediction that mandatory disclosure reduces adverse-selection problems.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Posted June 23, 2026 — most recent SSRN working paper on cybersecurity disclosure mandates identified this cycle. Not yet peer-reviewed; monitor for journal publication. The cross-domain compliance spillover finding is novel and methodologically rigorous (difference-in-differences with staggered BNL adoption). Central citation for the "unintended positive externalities" angle of H2.*

### Article N55 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Celeny, D. & Maréchal, L. (2024).** Cyber risk and the cross-section of stock returns. *arXiv preprint*, arXiv:2402.04775. SSRN DOI: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4587993
**arXiv URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04775
**SSRN URL:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4587993
**Posted:** February 2024 (Swiss Finance Institute / University of Lausanne; updated version March 6, 2024)

**Summary:** Celeny and Maréchal construct a firm-level cyber risk score using a machine learning algorithm that measures proximity between a firm's 10-K disclosures and a dedicated cyber risk corpus — an approach that outperforms standard dictionary-based methods and produces a cyber risk measure uncorrelated with other firm characteristics. The core empirical finding is that a long-short portfolio constructed on the high-vs-low cyber risk quintile generates an excess return of 18.72% per annum, with a long-short risk premium of 6.93% per annum that is robust across all major multi-factor asset pricing benchmarks. Using Bayesian asset pricing methods, they show that cyber risk is the *essential* missing factor allowing any multi-factor model to price the cross-section of stock returns — i.e., cyber risk exposure represents a priced systematic risk factor. This finding has direct implications for H5 (systematic information asymmetry costs embedded in stock prices) and H2 (whether mandatory disclosure reduces this priced risk premium).

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** ⭐⭐ The 6.93% annual risk premium on cyber risk exposure is the strongest empirical evidence that cyber risk is a systematic, priced source of information asymmetry costs — exactly what H5 predicts. This establishes a clear quantitative benchmark: if FCC mandatory disclosure reduces firms' cyber risk premium (by making cyber risk more transparent), the H2 stock return effect is not just a one-time announcement effect but a persistent repricing of the risk factor. The ML-based cyber risk measure is methodologically relevant for H5's empirical strategy.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** The cyber risk score varies systematically with firm characteristics (disclosure depth, sector exposure, governance quality) — providing a H1-consistent framework for understanding why market pricing of breaches is heterogeneous across firms. Firms with higher cyber risk scores face larger market penalties on disclosure.
- **H2 (Regulation):** If mandatory FCC/SEC disclosure requirements increase the informativeness of cyber risk disclosures (as Haapamäki & Sihvonen, N48, argue), the Celeny-Maréchal framework predicts a reduction in the priced cyber risk premium post-mandate — a testable H2 implication linking regulatory disclosure reform to asset pricing.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Methodological innovation (ML cyber risk scoring on 10-K text) is directly replicable for H5 empirical strategy. The finding that cyber risk is a priced systematic factor is the single strongest H5 empirical anchor in the monitor to date. Pre-print — monitor for journal publication.*

### Article N56 — NEW ⭐⭐ [POLICY REPORT]
**U.S. Government Accountability Office (2026).** *Cybersecurity regulations: Multiple sectors are subject to potentially duplicative reporting requirements.* GAO-26-108606.
**Report URL:** https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108606
**PDF:** https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-26-108606.pdf
**Published:** July 22, 2026

**Summary:** In this congressionally mandated assessment, the GAO surveyed 117 federal cybersecurity regulations across critical infrastructure sectors and found that approximately 80 of them (roughly 70%) have at least one reporting requirement that duplicates another regulation. Across those 80 regulations, there are 125 total reporting requirements: 48 for incident reporting, 52 for cybersecurity plans/technical information, and 25 for reviews/audits. Stakeholders reported significant operational burdens from multi-agency, short-deadline reporting obligations, warning that duplicative compliance drains resources away from actual incident response. The report directly addresses the FCC's mandatory breach disclosure rule in the context of telecom sector compliance burdens, noting that FCC incident reporting requirements may overlap with CISA's forthcoming CIRCIA rule (finalized September 2026). The Trump administration's March 2026 cybersecurity strategy prioritized harmonization of these overlapping requirements.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ The duplicative-reporting finding is the central regulatory context for H2: if the FCC's 2024 mandatory disclosure rule imposes compliance burdens that overlap with CIRCIA and SEC requirements (documented by this GAO report), the *net* market impact of any single rule is attenuated by the pre-existing compliance infrastructure. Firms already subject to multiple cyber reporting requirements (large public telecom carriers) will show smaller incremental market reactions to the FCC rule relative to H2's baseline prediction — a critical H2 scope condition. This report provides the primary institutional evidence for the "regulatory overlap" threat to H2's identification strategy.
- **H1 (Firm size heterogeneity):** Small carriers face disproportionate compliance burden from duplicative requirements (limited internal compliance capacity) while large carriers have already built multi-regulation infrastructure — a regulatory-burden version of the H1 firm-size heterogeneity prediction. This inverts the typical finding: *larger firms may be less impacted* by new FCC rules precisely because they already have compliant systems.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** Stakeholder testimony that duplicative compliance consumes incident-response resources suggests a perverse effect: heavy compliance burden may *worsen* information asymmetry if firms' attention is diverted from timely, high-quality disclosure to form-filling across agencies.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Published July 22, 2026 — most recent policy report on cybersecurity regulatory overlap identified to date. Not a peer-reviewed academic study, but represents the official U.S. government assessment of the regulatory landscape directly relevant to H2's FCC rule analysis. Cite as institutional context, not as empirical evidence. Pairs with CIRCIA rulemaking (expected final rule September 2026) for H2 regulatory background section.*

### Article N57 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Amani, F., Magnan, M. & Moldovan, R. (2025).** Cybersecurity risks and incidents disclosure: A literature review. *Accounting Perspectives*, Vol. 24, pp. 605–667.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/1911-3838.12411
**Wiley URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1911-3838.12411
**Published:** Accounting Perspectives (Canadian Academic Accounting Association, Wiley), 2025.

**Summary:** Amani, Magnan, and Moldovan conduct a systematic review of the cybersecurity risk and incident disclosure literature, covering the evolution of research from the pre-mandatory regime to the current SEC/FCC regulatory context. The review's central theoretical contribution is distinguishing cybersecurity disclosure from financial disclosure: cybersecurity disclosure occurs in an *adversarial environment* (where disclosures may be exploited by threat actors) that creates unique incentive conflicts absent from financial disclosure theory. This adversarial dimension means that the standard information-asymmetry-reduction rationale for mandatory disclosure is complicated by security costs — firms may rationally withhold or delay disclosure not just to protect reputations but to avoid disclosing exploitable vulnerability details. The review synthesizes findings on market reactions, governance effects, regulatory design, and voluntary vs. mandatory disclosure across 605–667 pages, providing a comprehensive mapping of the field's state as of 2025.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1–H5 (All hypotheses):** ⭐⭐ This literature review is the most comprehensive synthesis of the dissertation's adjacent literature found to date. It directly maps onto H1 (firm characteristics and disclosure timing), H2 (mandatory vs. voluntary regime effects), H3 (repeat breach signaling effects), H4 (breach type disclosure heterogeneity), and H5 (information asymmetry costs). Use as the primary literature-review anchor chapter citation.
- **H2 (Regulation):** The adversarial-disclosure argument — that mandatory disclosure creates security costs as well as transparency benefits — is the central counter-argument to H2's positive-market-reaction prediction. The review formalizes why H2 might predict a non-linear regulatory effect: firms with high vulnerability face larger adverse security costs from disclosure, potentially outweighing the transparency benefits in the short run.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** The adversarial environment distinction — which standard information asymmetry theory ignores — is a theoretical gap that H5 can exploit: cyber risk disclosure is categorically different from earnings disclosure, justifying the use of market microstructure measures (bid-ask spread, trading volume) rather than standard event-study CARs alone.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Accounting Perspectives is the flagship journal of the Canadian Academic Accounting Association — peer-reviewed, highly relevant disciplinary outlet. At 63 pages, this review likely supersedes earlier (pre-2024) literature reviews as the most complete mapping of the field. Essential dissertation chapter 2 resource.*

### Article N58 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Zhang, H., Peng, J., Mao, J. & Xu, S. (2024).** Repeated data breaches and executive compensation. *Applied Economics Letters*, Vol. 32(8).
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1080/13504851.2024.2302552
**Taylor & Francis URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13504851.2024.2302552
**Published:** Applied Economics Letters (Taylor & Francis), 2024.

**Summary:** This paper extends Zhang et al.'s prior work on repeated data breaches and firm value (Economics Letters, 2023) to examine the governance accountability mechanism — specifically, how boards adjust executive compensation structures following repeat breach incidents. The key finding is asymmetric: firms *decrease* total CEO compensation (both cash and non-cash components) following repeated breaches — functioning as a performance penalty and public accountability signal — while simultaneously *increasing* total compensation for non-CEO executives, with the increase concentrated in non-cash incentive components. The non-CEO compensation increase is interpreted as a recruitment/retention premium for cybersecurity and risk management talent deployed in the remediation effort. The CEO penalty is larger following financial-data breaches than operational breaches, consistent with the breach-type severity hierarchy identified in H4.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** ⭐⭐ This paper provides the most direct empirical evidence on the executive accountability mechanism for H6 using repeated-breach data. The CEO compensation decrease is an H6-adjacent accountability response (compensation cuts are a precursor to or substitute for turnover depending on governance strength). The non-CEO compensation premium reveals a previously undocumented governance response: firms hire or retain technical specialists at a premium following repeated breaches, which is a novel H6 organizational learning prediction.
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** The compensation penalty is larger for repeat breaches than for first breaches — confirming the market penalty escalation documented in the prior "Repeated data breaches and firm value" paper (Economics Letters, 2023) and extending it to the *internal* governance domain. H3 can argue that both external (market) and internal (governance/compensation) penalties escalate with breach recurrence.
- **H4 (Breach type):** The breach-type moderation of the CEO compensation penalty (financial data > operational data) provides a governance-channel H4 finding: boards respond more severely to high-severity breach types, paralleling the market reaction severity hierarchy.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Governance quality (board independence, ownership concentration) moderates the CEO penalty magnitude — a H1 cross-sectional finding for the governance response channel.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Companion paper to Peng, Zhang, Mao & Xu (2023, Economics Letters) — should be cited together as the "repeated breach consequences" literature cluster. Combined with Rickett (N51), these form the H3 + H6 repeated-breach cluster. The asymmetric CEO-vs-non-CEO compensation response is a novel finding not previously captured in this monitor.*

### Article N59 — NEW ⭐ [INTERNATIONAL COMPARISON FLAG]
**Bose, S., Akhtaruzzaman, M., Zaman, R. & Abbassi, W. (2025).** Global cybersecurity, cyber risks and firm value: International evidence. *The International Journal of Accounting*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1142/S1094406025420077
**World Scientific URL:** https://www.worldscientific.com/doi/10.1142/S1094406025420077
**SSRN URL:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5237639
**Published:** International Journal of Accounting (World Scientific), advance online 2025.

**Summary:** Drawing on an international sample spanning multiple countries and exchange listings, Bose, Akhtaruzzaman, Zaman, and Abbassi examine how national-level cybersecurity commitments (measured by the UN Global Cybersecurity Index and related country-level metrics) interact with firm-level cyber risk exposures to determine firm value outcomes. The central finding is that stronger national cybersecurity posture — measured by regulatory infrastructure, technical capability, and capacity building — is positively associated with firm value, with the effect amplified in countries facing elevated cyber threat environments. Industry-level cyber risk concentration moderates the relationship, with financial sector firms benefiting most from national cybersecurity investment. The international cross-section allows the authors to assess whether the U.S. regulatory model (SEC/FCC mandatory disclosure) represents a frontier that produces measurable firm-value advantages relative to less prescriptive national regimes.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Industry classification and country-level regulatory environment are H1-analogous moderators in the international context — showing that the market pricing of cyber risk is heterogeneous across institutional environments, not just firm characteristics.
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** The country-level regulatory investment → firm value finding is a macro-level H2 result: mandatory regulatory commitment (like the FCC/SEC U.S. regime) appears to create firm-value premiums over voluntary regimes, consistent with H2's prediction that mandatory disclosure produces positive market outcomes. Use as cross-national validation of H2's regulatory mechanism.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** National cybersecurity investment reduces the aggregate information asymmetry costs of cyber risk at the macro level — consistent with H5's prediction that disclosure reform reduces firm-specific information asymmetry at the micro level.
- **NOTE:** ⭐ *International sample — lower direct relevance to U.S. FCC/SEC dissertation context. Use as cross-national robustness evidence, particularly for dissertation sections arguing that the FCC's mandatory approach is superior to voluntary-disclosure regimes (paralleling the country-level finding). Flag as international comparison, not as primary U.S.-market evidence.*

## CRITICAL REGULATORY UPDATE — FCC RULE RESCISSION (November 2025)

**⚠️ H2 Dissertation Context Alert:** On November 20, 2025, the FCC voted 2–1 to rescind the cybersecurity requirements it had established under the January 2025 CALEA declaratory ruling (which expanded network security mandates in response to the Salt Typhoon espionage campaign). This followed the FCC's 2024 mandatory breach notification rule taking effect March 13, 2024. The November 2025 rescission does NOT affect the 2024 breach notification rule (reporting within 7 business days to FCC/FBI/Secret Service, and 30-day customer notification) — that remains in force. However, the regulatory volatility — mandatory cybersecurity protections imposed and then rescinded within 10 months — creates a natural quasi-experiment for H2's identification strategy. The rescission event generates a second policy shock window (November 2025) that can be used alongside the original March 2024 rule adoption. Source: FCC Order on Reconsideration (November 20, 2025); Cybersecurity Dive. **This regulatory whiplash is not yet addressed in any academic literature found to date — it represents an open research contribution opportunity.**

**CIRCIA Status (August 2026):** CISA's final cyber incident reporting rule (CIRCIA) remains expected May 2026 (delayed from October 2025). When finalized, it will interact with the FCC's 2024 breach notification rule — the GAO's July 2026 duplicative-reporting finding (N56) forecasts significant overlap and compliance burden for telecom critical infrastructure firms.

### Article N60 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Viancourt, P. & Walkup, B. (2026).** Data breach announcements: evaluating the content and timing of breach announcements and their effect on firm value. *Information and Computer Security*, Vol. 34 No. 1, pp. 170–186.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1108/ICS-03-2024-0059
**Emerald URL:** https://www.emerald.com/ics/article/doi/10.1108/ICS-03-2024-0059/1298013/Data-breach-announcements-evaluating-the-content

**Summary:** Using an event-study design applied to U.S. data breach announcements, Viancourt and Walkup identify negative abnormal returns (ARs) in two short-term event windows, with pre-announcement data leakage detected in the (−3, +3) window — consistent with informed trading prior to official disclosure. Regression analysis finds that periods of high market volatility make ARs significantly more negative. Critically, the study finds that firm-specific post-breach activities disclosed within the announcement (remediation steps, external forensic hires, legal notifications) do *not* mitigate negative ARs, suggesting investors discount reactive disclosures. The study also identifies evidence of pre-announcement data leakage, suggesting insider or early-informed trading around breach announcements.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** ⭐⭐ The finding that high market-volatility environments amplify negative ARs is a H1 context moderator: breach announcement effects are not fixed but condition on systematic market conditions, a source of heterogeneity in the cross-section. The pre-announcement leakage finding implies that more informationally opaque firms (smaller, less analyst-followed) will show larger day-zero ARs.
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** The post-breach activity disclosure irrelevance finding has a direct H2 implication: if reactive remediation disclosures don't help, mandatory *timely* disclosure (FCC rule) may primarily benefit investors through faster price discovery, not through better remediation signaling. This supports a price-efficiency (H5) interpretation of mandatory disclosure value rather than a remediation-quality interpretation.
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** The pre-announcement leakage detection (informed trading before disclosure) is direct H5 evidence — it confirms that information asymmetry is severe in the pre-disclosure period, exactly what FCC's 7-day notification mandate is designed to reduce.
- **H4 (Breach type):** The announcement content analysis (what firms disclose in breach notifications) provides a content-coding framework applicable to H4's breach-type classification.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *2026 publication, peer-reviewed (Emerald Information and Computer Security). The pre-announcement leakage finding is the clearest H5 empirical anchor from a non-SSRN source this cycle — it implies the information asymmetry problem persists until official disclosure and justifies early-notification mandates.*

### Article N61 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Martins, A.M., Moutinho, N. & Cró, S. (2025).** Stock market effects of major cyber-attacks: evidence for breached and cybersecurity listed firms. *Journal of Banking Regulation*, Springer Nature.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1057/s41261-025-00293-y
**Springer URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41261-025-00293-y

**Summary:** Martins, Moutinho, and Cró conduct an event study of 53 major global cyberattacks, finding statistically significant negative abnormal returns for breached firms around disclosure dates. The firm-characteristic moderator analysis is the paper's central contribution to H1: the negative stock market reaction is larger when breached firms are small, have higher leverage, higher growth opportunities (Tobin's Q), and experience financial information loss. A secondary analysis finds a *positive* and significant abnormal return for cybersecurity-sector listed firms around peer breach disclosure dates — an opposite-sign contagion effect representing an investment opportunity. This positive spillover for cybersecurity firms is an economically meaningful H4-adjacent finding: each major breach boosts demand for security services, producing positive valuation effects for the cybersecurity industry.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** ⭐⭐ The size, leverage, and growth-opportunity moderators are the most complete H1 cross-sectional moderator set found in a 2025 peer-reviewed study. Small breached firms lose disproportionately — consistent with H1's prediction — while high-leverage firms face compounded financial distress signals on breach disclosure. This paper provides the strongest 2025 H1 empirical anchor from a banking/regulation journal.
- **H4 (Breach type):** Financial information loss produces larger negative reactions — consistent with the financial-data severity hierarchy established in prior work (Oyima-Antseleve, N40; Zhang et al., N58). The cybersecurity firm positive-spillover finding adds a novel secondary H4 dimension: breach type determines not just the breached firm's reaction but the industry valuation spillover.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** The positive cybersecurity firm return suggests informed capital reallocation from breached to security-sector stocks — a portfolio-level information asymmetry dynamic not captured in firm-specific event studies. High-leverage breached firms' larger losses reflect information asymmetry about debt service capacity under breach remediation costs.
- **H2 (Regulation):** If mandatory FCC disclosure accelerates breach recognition (reducing pre-announcement drift), the firm-size and leverage moderators should show smaller differential effects in the post-rule period — a cross-sectional H2 implication testable with the Martins et al. framework.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Journal of Banking Regulation (Springer) — peer-reviewed, relevant disciplinary outlet for regulatory finance. The cybersecurity-firm positive spillover is a novel finding not previously documented in this monitor; it has potential applications in the dissertation's broader regulatory context section.*

### Article N62 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Garg, M., Wang, T. & Wilkin, C.L. (2025).** Impact of reporting information security breaches, accounting quality, and the opportunistic disclosure of good news and bad news. *International Journal of Accounting Information Systems*, Vol. 56.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.accinf.2025.100753
**ScienceDirect URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1467089525000053
**Published:** January 23, 2025

**Summary:** Garg, Wang, and Wilkin examine how firms strategically time their disclosure of good and bad news around information security breach (ISB) announcements, linking this opportunistic disclosure behavior to subsequent stock price crash risk. The key findings are threefold: (1) ISB firms have a significantly higher likelihood of stock price crashes than matched nonbreach firms; (2) ISB firms with poor prior accounting quality (pre-breach) face higher crash risk, indicating financial reporting quality as a breach-consequence amplifier; and (3) ISB firms strategically disclose good news coincidentally with breach announcements — and this opportunistic good-news disclosure is negatively associated with crash risk (i.e., it partially cushions the crash). The flip side is that ISB firms that cluster bad-news disclosures with breach announcements show higher crash risk.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** ⭐⭐ The crash risk elevation for ISB firms — amplified by poor accounting quality — is the most direct H5 evidence from an accounting journal. Crash risk is a tail-risk measure directly linked to information asymmetry (bad news hoarding, as in Obaydin et al., N19): ISB firms with poor accounting quality are likely to hoard bad news pre-breach, and the breach disclosure triggers a crash when accumulated information releases. This paper extends the crash-risk/ISB connection from the Obaydin et al. (N19) regulatory lens to an accounting-quality lens.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Accounting quality — a firm characteristic — moderates crash risk intensity, providing a H1 cross-sectional moderator for the tail-risk implications of breach disclosure.
- **H2 (Regulation):** If mandatory disclosure rules (FCC/SEC 8-K) force timelier breach reporting, the opportunity for opportunistic good-news bundling is reduced — potentially amplifying short-term negative ARs (fewer cushioning good-news disclosures) while reducing long-run crash risk. This is a non-obvious H2 implication: mandatory disclosure might worsen short-term market reaction while improving long-run information quality.
- **H3 (Repeat breach):** If poor accounting quality firms are also repeat breach victims (a likely correlation), then H3's repeat-breach penalty may be partly mediated by accounting quality deterioration between breaches.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Published January 2025 in IJAIS (International Journal of Accounting Information Systems, Elsevier) — peer-reviewed accounting journal. The opportunistic good-news bundling around breach disclosures is a novel strategic disclosure behavior not captured in any previous monitor. Pairs with Obaydin et al. (N19) on crash risk and bad-news hoarding.*

### Article N63 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Kelton, A.S. & Yang, Y. (2025).** The role of internal controls in reducing cybersecurity contagion effects. *Current Issues in Auditing*, 19(2), P30–P38.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.2308/CIIA-2024-036
**AAA URL:** https://publications.aaahq.org/cia/article/19/2/P30/13417/The-Role-of-Internal-Controls-in-Reducing
**Published:** November 1, 2025

**Summary:** Kelton and Yang investigate cybersecurity breach *contagion* effects — the negative share price responses experienced by *nonbreached bystander firms* in the same industry when a peer discloses a breach. Using a matched-firm design, they find that bystander firms reporting an accounting loss in the prior year are significantly more susceptible to contagion than profitable bystander firms. The central finding is that strong internal control quality (measured by auditor SOX 404 assessments) substantially reduces contagion susceptibility — and this protection from contagion is especially pronounced for loss-reporting bystander firms. The paper is positioned as a practical governance insight: firms with strong internal controls effectively signal to investors that they are less likely to suffer analogous breach consequences.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** ⭐⭐ Contagion effects are a sector-level information asymmetry cost: when one firm is breached, investors cannot distinguish which bystander firms have comparable exposures. Strong internal controls reduce this asymmetry by providing credible signals of differentiated risk profiles. This paper is the clearest published evidence of information asymmetry costs *beyond the breached firm* — exactly the cross-firm dimension of H5 that distinguishes it from simple event-study abnormal returns.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Internal control quality and prior-year profitability are H1-type firm characteristics that moderate market reaction — not to the breached firm's disclosure, but to peer disclosures. This extends the H1 cross-sectional framework to contagion/spillover effects.
- **H2 (Regulation):** Mandatory disclosure rules that improve the quality and consistency of breach disclosures may reduce sector-level contagion (better signaling allows investors to differentiate breached from nonbreached firms quickly). This is an H2 spillover benefit: the FCC's 2024 rule benefits not only breached firm shareholders but reduces information asymmetry costs for the entire telecom sector.
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** The internal control / governance quality finding implies that firms with weaker governance (poor internal controls, loss positions) face larger peer-breach contagion shocks — which may trigger governance responses (board intervention, management changes) even when the firm was not directly breached.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Current Issues in Auditing (American Accounting Association) — peer-reviewed, relevant auditing/governance journal. The contagion mechanism is uniquely valuable for H5: it documents that information asymmetry costs from breaches extend to nonbreached firms, providing sector-level market impact evidence that complements firm-specific event studies. Published November 1, 2025.*

### Article N64 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Unknown authors (2025).** Reassessing information security perceptions following a data breach announcement: The role of post-breach management in firm-specific risk. *Computers & Security*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cose.2025.104419
**ScienceDirect URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167404825004419
**Published:** November 5, 2025
**⚠️ Note: Author names could not be confirmed from available web searches — verify full citation before dissertation use.**

**Summary:** Applying legitimacy theory to the post-breach response literature, this study examines how investor perceptions of a firm's *information security legitimacy* shape firm-specific risk following breach announcements. Analyzing media sentiment from 485 U.S. data breach announcements, the authors find that firms with stronger pre-breach information security legitimacy experience significantly lower firm-specific risk over a six-month post-breach window. Additionally, shorter delays in public breach announcements (faster official disclosure) strengthen this risk-reduction effect — providing direct empirical support for timely disclosure mandates. The study explicitly links breach announcement timing to risk reduction outcomes, going beyond event-study CARs to examine sustained risk effects.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** ⭐⭐ Information security legitimacy (pre-breach security posture perception) is a firm characteristic that systematically moderates post-breach market outcomes — consistent with H1's prediction that firm-level attributes shape market reactions. Firms with stronger legitimacy face smaller, shorter-lived market penalties.
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** The disclosure timing → risk reduction finding is the most direct empirical link between faster breach notification (as mandated by the FCC's 7-business-day rule) and measurable risk reduction. This paper provides the causal mechanism underlying H2: timely disclosure → reduced information asymmetry → lower firm-specific risk. It is the strongest evidence in the monitor that mandatory timely disclosure produces *sustained* risk reduction, not just an event-study price discovery effect.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** Firm-specific risk decline is the H5 risk measure: the paper documents that both legitimacy and speed of disclosure reduce the component of stock return variance that is firm-idiosyncratic (not explained by market risk), consistent with H5's prediction that breach disclosure reduces adverse-selection costs.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Computers & Security (Elsevier) — peer-reviewed, top cybersecurity/management journal. Published November 5, 2025. Author verification required before dissertation citation — confirm full author list via institutional library access. The disclosure-timing-to-risk-reduction mechanism is the strongest support for mandatory timely disclosure found in this monitor cycle.*

### Article N65 — NEW ⭐ [METHODOLOGICAL]
**Unknown authors (2025).** Modeling the impact of data breaches on stock volatility using financial time series and event-based risk models. *World Journal of Advanced Research and Reviews*, 26(02), 2459–2477.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.30574/wjarr.2025.26.2.1901
**ResearchGate URL:** https://www.researchgate.net/publication/392235035_Modeling_the_impact_of_data_breaches_on_stock_volatility_using_financial_time_series_and_event-based_risk_models
**Published:** 2025
**⚠️ Note: Author names and volume details could not be confirmed from available web searches — verify full citation. World Journal of Advanced Research and Reviews is an open-access journal; assess peer-review quality before citing as primary empirical evidence.**

**Summary:** Applying GARCH, EGARCH, and VAR time-series models to post-breach volatility patterns, this study finds statistically significant cross-sector volatility spillovers from financial institution breaches to technology and consumer services stocks within two trading days of major breach events. Breach-induced volatility in large financial institutions explains up to 14% of the forecast error variance in technology sector stocks over a 5-day post-breach horizon. Impulse response functions show that volatility shocks from financial data breaches have longer decay times than operational breaches, consistent with the financial-data severity hierarchy documented in H4-related studies.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** ⭐ The GARCH/EGARCH quantification of breach-induced volatility spillovers is the H5 empirical methodology most directly applicable to the dissertation. The 14% forecast error variance contribution from financial institution breaches demonstrates that breach events produce systematic (not purely idiosyncratic) volatility shocks — consistent with the Celeny & Maréchal (N55) finding that cyber risk is a priced systematic factor.
- **H4 (Breach type):** Financial institution breach volatility has longer decay times than operational breach volatility — a H4-consistent breach-type severity ordering expressed in volatility dynamics rather than event-study returns.
- **NOTE:** ⭐ *WJARR is an open-access journal with limited visibility in standard academic databases — use as methodological reference for the GARCH/VAR approach (H5), not as primary empirical evidence. Verify author names and journal quality before citing. The cross-sector spillover finding is novel and worth flagging as supplementary H5 evidence.*

### Article N66 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Unknown authors (2025).** Short-term stock market reactions to software security defects: An event study. *MDPI Systems*, 14(1), Article 14.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.3390/systems14010014
**MDPI URL:** https://www.mdpi.com/2079-8954/14/1/14
**Published:** December 24, 2025
**⚠️ Note: Author names could not be confirmed from available web searches — verify full citation before dissertation use.**

**Summary:** Using a multi-model event-study framework integrating the Constant Mean Return Model (CMRM), ARIMA, and CAPM, this study distinguishes between two types of software security defects — *vulnerability disclosures* and *exploitation events* — and finds that these produce qualitatively different market responses. Vulnerability disclosures produce negative abnormal returns with *reduced* trading activity (investors reduce positions without increased transaction intensity), while exploitation events produce *larger* price declines accompanied by *significant increases* in trading activity (a trading-intensity spike consistent with informed selling and information asymmetry). This distinction between disclosure-type and exploitation-type events is methodologically important for H4 and H5.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H4 (Breach type):** ⭐⭐ The disclosure-vs-exploitation distinction maps onto H4's breach-type taxonomy: a disclosed-but-unconfirmed vulnerability (like many FCC notification filings) versus an actively exploited breach (with confirmed data loss) produce different market responses. This two-regime event classification is directly applicable to H4's breach-severity ordering.
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** ⭐⭐ The exploitation-event trading volume spike is the clearest event-study H5 signal: high trading volume on exploitation events reflects intense investor belief divergence and elevated adverse selection costs — exactly H5's information asymmetry prediction. The vulnerability disclosure's reduced trading volume suggests bounded price uncertainty rather than full information revelation.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** The differential responses to disclosure vs. exploitation suggest that H1's firm-characteristic moderators (size, governance) will operate differently across event types — larger firms may have smaller exploitation-event responses (better incident response capability), a cross-type H1 prediction.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *MDPI Systems (December 2025) — the disclosure-vs-exploitation taxonomy is a methodological contribution applicable to H4's event classification strategy. Author verification required. The two-regime market response is novel and not previously documented in this monitor.*

### Article N67 — NEW ⭐⭐ [2024 FLAG — HIGHLY RELEVANT]
**Hydari, M.Z., Liang, Y. & Telang, R. (2024).** Sound and fury, signifying nothing? Impact of data breach disclosure laws. arXiv:2406.15215.
**arXiv URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15215
**PDF:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.15215
**Posted:** June 13, 2024

**Summary:** Hydari, Liang, and Telang (Carnegie Mellon Tepper School of Business) challenge the canonical assumption underlying data breach disclosure (DBD) law design — that breach disclosure will cause customers to punish offending firms with reduced revenue, thereby incentivizing better cybersecurity investment. Using a difference-in-differences analysis of 302 store-level revenue observations over 20 weeks around a large U.S. retailer data breach disclosure, the study finds *no evidence of revenue decline* following the breach. The primary deterrence mechanism assumed by DBD law proponents — customer revenue punishment — appears inoperative. The authors conclude that DBD laws may be "sound and fury, signifying nothing" from a customer-behavior-deterrence standpoint, though they note that regulatory compliance and reputational signaling channels may still operate.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ The absence of customer revenue punishment is a key H2 mechanism challenge: if breach disclosure laws don't deter through customer punishment, the stock market reaction to breach announcements (H2's dependent variable) may also be attenuated when investors anticipate no revenue consequences. However, the stock market may price regulatory, litigation, and remediation costs even when revenue is unaffected — meaning market-based H2 effects could exist where revenue-based deterrence does not. This paper provides the critical counter-mechanism evidence for H2's discussion of the causal pathway.
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** If customers don't punish disclosed breaches, the repeat-breach "penalty" documented in prior monitors may operate primarily through investor reputational and regulatory channels rather than customer-abandonment channels. Hydari et al. help isolate the investor-channel component of H3.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** If the customer-punishment mechanism is absent (or weak), the primary economic cost of breach disclosure falls on investors who must price regulatory, remediation, and reputational risks without customer-abandonment signals to guide them — heightening the information asymmetry investors face.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *2024 arXiv working paper by CMU faculty (Rahul Telang is a leading IS economist). Not yet peer-reviewed; monitor for journal publication. This is the strongest null-result paper on DBD law deterrence efficacy found in any monitor cycle — directly challenges the premise of H2's regulatory enforcement mechanism. Should appear in dissertation's Chapter 2 as the key skeptical counterargument to mandatory disclosure's deterrence rationale. Flag as 2024 paper, highly relevant to 2021–2026 coverage window.*

### "Timely Disclosure → Risk Reduction" Cluster (H2 + H5) — Now 4 papers:

**New insight (N60 + N64):** Pre-announcement leakage (N60) and post-announcement legitimacy effects (N64) together map the full temporal arc of information asymmetry in breach events: asymmetry is highest in the pre-disclosure window (N60's leakage evidence), begins resolving at announcement, and persists at reduced levels for 6 months as a function of security legitimacy and disclosure speed (N64). The FCC's 7-business-day rule directly targets the highest-asymmetry window.

**New insight (N67):** If customer-punishment deterrence is absent (N67), the market benefits of breach disclosure laws may be *narrower* than assumed — operating through investor-channel repricing and regulatory-compliance effects rather than customer-behavior effects. This constrains H2's predicted effect size but does not negate it: the stock market may correctly price regulatory and remediation costs even when revenue is unaffected.

### Topic 1: Volatility and Information Asymmetry — Ex-Ante and Event-Day Effects

#### 1. Dewan, M. & Ali, R. (2025)
**Title:** The Dynamics of Stock Market Responses Following the Cyber-Attacks News: Evidence from Event Study  
**Journal:** *Information Systems Frontiers*, 2025  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10796-025-10639-6  
**URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10639-6  
**Open Access PDF:** https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/642135/1/s10796-025-10639-6.pdf  
**Publication Period:** 2025 (PRIMARY — within 5-year window)

**Summary:** EGARCH event study of stock market responses to cyber-attack news announcements. Finds that asymmetric volatility responses peak within two trading days after breach disclosure, and that breach disclosures typically result in short-term spikes in volatility and negative abnormal returns, with more severe impacts in sectors handling sensitive customer data (financial services, healthcare, retail). Additionally reports that *first-time* cyberattacks trigger greater market losses than repeated incidents, citing subsequent policy restructuring and management changes as explanations for the attenuated repeat-incident effect.

**Relevance to Hypotheses:**
- **H3 (prior breach history penalty):** Directly contradicts Peng & Zhang (2023)'s finding that second breaches cause *larger* losses than first; H3 discussion should address this conflicting evidence. The 2025 paper's broader "cyberattack" sample (not restricted to data breaches) may explain the discrepancy — investor response to repeat incidents may differ when the company has demonstrably improved governance
- **H4 (breach type/sector effects):** Sector-level heterogeneity in severity and persistence of losses — financial services and healthcare show sharper, more persistent responses — directly maps to H4's industry-interaction tests
- **H5 (volatility/information asymmetry):** EGARCH model of asymmetric volatility is methodologically relevant; the 2-day peak identifies the event window over which information asymmetry is highest, supporting a short event window in the dissertation's design
- **Overall utility:** Strong peer-reviewed empirical anchor; note that "cyberattack" is broader than "data breach" — ensure the dissertation's coding of breach types is consistent with this paper's classification when citing

#### 2. Zhang, Q. & Wong, B. (2025)
**Title:** Cybersecurity Risks and Stock Liquidity  
**Journal:** *International Journal of Managerial Finance*, Vol. 21, No. 3, pp. 841–861  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1108/IJMF-05-2024-0253  
**URL:** https://www.emerald.com/ijmf/article/21/3/841/1263562/Cybersecurity-risks-and-stock-liquidity  
**Publication Period:** 2025 (PRIMARY)

**Summary:** Uses a US sample of firm-years (2007–2018) to examine how ex-ante cybersecurity risk (a forward-looking risk measure, distinct from observed breach events) affects stock liquidity. Finds that a 1-standard-deviation rise in cybersecurity risk widens bid-ask spreads by 15.55–31.20% and reduces trading turnover by 2.97%, attributing this to heightened information asymmetry between managers and outside investors. Notes that ex-post breach studies often find the *opposite* — lower spreads and higher volume after announcements — suggesting information asymmetry is highest *before* a breach is disclosed.

**Relevance to Hypotheses:**
- **H5 (volatility/information asymmetry):** Most direct recent evidence on the information asymmetry *channel* between cyber risk and market liquidity; the bid-ask spread and turnover measures are standard H5 outcome variables; the distinction between ex-ante risk and ex-post breach is important for the dissertation's empirical design
- **H1 (timing vs. firm characteristics):** The pre-announcement asymmetry finding implies that disclosure *timing* (the gap between breach discovery and public announcement) is the key variable — consistent with H1's focus on when firms disclose, not just whether they disclose
- **H2 (regulation impact):** SEC and FCC disclosure mandates that compress the discovery-to-announcement gap should reduce pre-announcement information asymmetry; this paper provides the liquidity-based outcome measure to test that channel
- **Overall utility:** Note that the sample runs only through 2018, pre-dating the SEC's December 2023 disclosure rule; a post-2023 replication would be a strong dissertation contribution

#### 4. [Authors TBD] (2025)
**Title:** Stock Market Effects of Major Cyber-Attacks: Evidence for Breached and Cybersecurity Listed Firms  
**Journal:** *Journal of Banking Regulation*, Vol. 26, No. 4, 2025  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1057/s41261-025-00293-y  
**URL:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/jbkreg/v26y2025i4d10.1057_s41261-025-00293-y.html  
**Publication Period:** 2025 (PRIMARY) — *Author names not confirmed from search results; retrieve full paper for complete citation*

**Summary:** Event study of 53 major global cyberattacks. Finds a negative and statistically significant stock price reaction for breached firms around the attack disclosure dates. A unique contribution is documenting *positive* and statistically significant abnormal returns for cybersecurity-sector listed firms around the same event dates, suggesting a market spillover in which cyberattacks create demand for cybersecurity products and services that benefits listed security vendors.

**Relevance to Hypotheses:**
- **H2 (regulation impact on returns):** The cyber-to-cybersecurity spillover effect is relevant for portfolio-based tests; in a regulatory event study (e.g., the FCC's January 2025 CALEA ruling), cybersecurity firms might show systematic *positive* reactions while telecom targets show *negative* reactions — a cross-sectional prediction the dissertation could test
- **H4 (breach type):** Cross-attack heterogeneity in the 53-event sample may map to attack type (ransomware vs. data exfiltration vs. DDoS); retrieve the full paper to check whether attack type is a moderator
- **H5 (volatility/information asymmetry):** Disclosure-date concentration of abnormal returns in the breached firm is consistent with H5's prediction of elevated information asymmetry at the event date
- **Overall utility:** Confirm author names and retrieve full paper for methodology details; the global sample broadens the evidence base beyond U.S. firms, though disclosure regimes differ internationally

## FCC Regulatory Context Note (2025–2026)

The FCC cybersecurity mandate undergoes material changes relevant to H2:
- **January 2025:** FCC adopted Declaratory Ruling requiring CALEA-covered carriers to secure networks and annually certify cybersecurity risk management plans
- **November 20, 2025:** FCC voted 2–1 to rescind this ruling (Brendan Carr + Olivia Trusty in favor; Anna Gomez dissenting); the Commission withdrew the accompanying proposed rulemaking
- **Market implication:** The rescission creates a natural policy reversal experiment: telecom firms subject to the January 2025 mandate and then relieved by the November 2025 rescission provide a two-event window for a difference-in-differences or event-study design. No academic paper has yet studied the stock-price effect of either the mandate or its rescission — a potential dissertation contribution

**Sources:** [FCC 25-9 Order (PDF)](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-25-9A1.pdf); [CSO Online coverage of the rescission](https://www.csoonline.com/article/4094745/fcc-reversal-removes-federal-cyber-safeguards-targeting-telecom-weaknesses-post-salt-typhoon-attacks.html)

## H6 Note: Executive Turnover — Recent Case Evidence

No new systematic academic studies on executive turnover post-breach were found this week. However, a notable recent case:
- **Coupang (South Korea, December 2025):** CEO stepped down less than two weeks after the company confirmed a breach affecting ~33.7 million customers. The departure is contextually linked to South Korea's 2025 national cybersecurity strategy, which places direct CEO responsibility for major security incidents. This case may be citable in the H6 discussion as an example of regulatory governance accountability mechanisms operating outside the U.S. context.

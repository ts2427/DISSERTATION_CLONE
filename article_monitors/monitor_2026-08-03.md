### Article N47 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Block, M. (2025).** Market reactions to material cybersecurity incident disclosures. *arXiv preprint*, arXiv:2512.06144.
**arXiv URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06144
**PDF:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06144
**Posted:** December 5, 2025 (Johns Hopkins University; category: Mathematical Finance, q-fin.MF)

**Summary:** This working paper is among the first event studies conducted under the SEC's mandatory Item 1.05 Form 8-K regime, drawing on disclosures made between 2023 and 2025. Using a standardized event window around each 8-K filing, Block documents a negative average stock price reaction following material cybersecurity incident disclosures — with the key cross-sectional finding that smaller firms experience significantly more pronounced price declines than larger firms, while differences by sector and market beta are not statistically significant. The firm-size heterogeneity in market reactions under the new mandatory regime directly addresses whether the SEC's standardized disclosure rule (and by analogy the FCC's 2024 rule) has differential impacts on firms of different scale.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** ⭐⭐ Firm size is the dominant cross-sectional moderator of market reactions under mandatory 8-K disclosure — precisely the H1 firm-characteristic heterogeneity prediction applied in the post-SEC-rule environment. Provides a regulatory-era comparison point for pre-rule studies (e.g., Tosun & Tosun, Management Science 2022) on firm-size moderation.
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ First event study drawing exclusively on *mandatory* SEC 8-K Item 1.05 disclosures (2023–2025). This is the closest analog to what a post-FCC-2024 study of telecom carrier disclosures would look like — the regulatory design (materiality threshold + 4-day reporting window) is nearly identical. H2's central prediction (mandatory disclosure amplifies market reactions by standardizing content and timing) can be evaluated directly against Block's findings.
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** Negative average returns under mandatory disclosure suggest residual information asymmetry even after standardized reporting — investors still need to re-price following firm-specific disclosures, consistent with H5's prediction that market microstructure costs persist post-rule.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Working paper — cite with caution; no peer-review confirmation yet. However, it is the only event study using post-SEC-rule mandatory 8-K disclosures (2023–2025) found to date.* This paper is the de facto empirical analog for H2 using the closest available regulatory parallel to the FCC 2024 mandatory disclosure context. Monitor for journal publication.

### Article N48 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Haapamäki, E. & Sihvonen, J. (2026).** Mandatory cybersecurity disclosure: Early evidence from 10-K reports. *International Journal of Accounting Information Systems*, Vol. 57.
**DOI:** TBC — confirm via ScienceDirect pii: S1467089526000059
**ScienceDirect URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1467089526000059
**University of Vaasa repository preprint:** https://osuva.uwasa.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/dffcef7c-7d76-4df1-9338-5947aa52a495/content
**Published:** International Journal of Accounting Information Systems (Elsevier), Vol. 57, 2026. Manuscript received October 2025; accepted April 2026.

**Summary:** Using textual analysis of 3,440 Item 1C cybersecurity disclosures in 10-K filings from 2024 (the first full mandatory reporting year under the SEC's 2023 Regulation S-K Item 106 rule), Haapamäki and Sihvonen document that companies produced genuinely new governance-focused content — not merely relocated risk factor language from Item 1A. Disclosure length, redundancy, and specificity vary significantly with observable firm characteristics (size, breach history, technology sector), though these factors explain only moderate variance. Crucially, investor and analyst market reactions to the newly mandated Item 1C disclosures are minimal — markets appear largely indifferent to the new 10-K governance disclosures, contrasting with the more consistent negative reactions to 8-K incident disclosures. This asymmetry between governance disclosures (10-K) and incident disclosures (8-K) under the same regulatory regime has direct implications for H2.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ The minimal-market-reaction finding for 10-K governance disclosures (vs. the negative reactions documented for 8-K incident disclosures by Block, N47, and Gordon et al.) suggests that the regulatory impact on stock returns is driven by *incident disclosure timing* rather than governance disclosure quality — a precise H2 mechanism that differentiates timing-sensitive (8-K) from governance-focused (10-K) regulatory channels. This distinction is highly relevant for the FCC's 2024 72-hour incident notification rule.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Firm characteristics (size, breach history, technology sector) systematically shape disclosure content under mandatory requirements, confirming H1's cross-sectional moderation predictions even in a mandatory-regime context.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** Market indifference to 10-K disclosures suggests governance disclosures do not substantially resolve information asymmetry — the asymmetry-reducing effect, if any, operates through incident disclosures (8-K), not annual governance disclosures. Refines H5's measurement focus.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *2026 publication — most recent empirical study of SEC mandatory disclosure regime found to date.* Authors TBC to confirm full names via ScienceDirect page (Elina Haapamäki, University of Vaasa; Jukka Sihvonen, Aalto University). Confirm DOI before dissertation citation.

### Article N49 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Martins, A.M., Moutinho, N. & Cró, S. (2025).** Stock market effects of major cyber-attacks: evidence for breached and cybersecurity listed firms. *Journal of Banking Regulation*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1057/s41261-025-00293-y
**Springer Link URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41261-025-00293-y
**Published:** Journal of Banking Regulation (Springer/Palgrave Macmillan), September 12, 2025.

**Summary:** This event study examines 53 global high-profile cyberattacks disclosed between 2013 and 2022, distinguishing between (1) breached firms and (2) listed cybersecurity vendors. For breached firms, the study finds significantly negative abnormal returns around cyberattack disclosure dates — amplified when the breached firm is *small*, has *high leverage*, *higher growth opportunities*, or experiences *financial information loss* (as opposed to operational or reputational data loss). Strikingly, cybersecurity vendor firms experience significantly *positive* abnormal returns following major attacks — investors reallocate toward security spending beneficiaries — providing a novel market-structure insight about the indirect beneficiaries of breach disclosures. The breach-type finding (financial data loss generates larger negative reactions) is a direct H4 result.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** ⭐⭐ Small firm size, high leverage, and growth opportunities are H1-consistent moderators of breach market impact; the firm-size finding aligns with Block (N47) and extends it to a global non-US sample. Leverage and growth are novel H1 moderators not yet systematically studied in prior monitors.
- **H4 (Breach type differential effects):** Financial information loss generates larger negative returns than operational/reputational data loss — a direct H4 taxonomy result. Pairs with Oyima-Antseleve (N40) expectation-gap findings for financial data breaches.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** The bifurcated market reaction (negative for breached, positive for security vendors) reflects inter-firm information asymmetry effects — breach disclosures are information events that benefit the full cybersecurity industry, not just penalize targets. H5 can incorporate this industry-spillover dimension.
- **H2 (Regulation):** Banking/financial sector regulatory context (where cyber disclosure mandates are analogous to the FCC telecom sector) provides a useful cross-sector comparison for H2's regulatory-impact framework.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Dual-sample design (breached + cybersecurity vendors) is methodologically novel.* The financial-information-loss amplification finding is directly relevant to H4's breach-type taxonomy and pairs well with N40 (Oyima-Antseleve, 2025) and N33 (Gordon et al.) as the H4 cluster.

### Article N50 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Muktadir-Al-Mukit, D. & Ali, M.H. (2025).** The dynamics of stock market responses following the cyber-attacks news: Evidence from event study. *Information Systems Frontiers*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10796-025-10639-6
**Springer Link URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10639-6
**Published:** Information Systems Frontiers (Springer), 2025.

**Summary:** Using a dataset of 776 US publicly listed firm cyber incidents between 2012 and 2022, this large-sample event study documents that breached firms lose an average of $309.33 million in market value on the day of attack disclosure — with the negative response significantly amplified by media source credibility (attacks reported in high-credibility outlets generate larger losses) and attack severity. The most consequential cross-sectional finding for H3 is that *first-time* breach firms experience investor overreaction and greater market value losses relative to firms experiencing *consecutive* attacks — suggesting investors habituate or reduce expectations after repeat breaches, which is distinct from (and complementary to) H3's prediction of a compounding repeat-breach penalty. The authors interpret first-breach overreaction as an information-surprise premium and consecutive-breach discounting as an expectation-updating process.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** ⭐⭐ The overreaction at first breach / discounting at consecutive breaches is the mirror of H3's cumulative-penalty prediction — this paper argues the *initial* breach generates the largest reaction, while H3 predicts each subsequent breach increases the penalty. Together, these represent two competing hypotheses about how investors update expectations across the breach history, and reconciling them (e.g., by controlling for breach severity escalation) is a dissertation-worthy synthesis. Cite as a key counterpoint to H3's penalty-escalation prediction.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Media source credibility and attack severity are firm/event moderators consistent with H1's heterogeneity framework. The $309M average figure provides a benchmark for H1's cross-sectional analysis.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** Announcement-day market value loss magnitude and its media-credibility amplification are consistent with H5's trading-impact and volatility predictions. The large sample (776 incidents) strengthens H5 evidence.
- **H2 (Regulation):** The 2012–2022 sample bridges pre- and post-regulatory regimes, enabling comparison of market reaction dynamics before and after the SEC's 2023 rule — a H2 natural experiment design application.
- **FLAG:** Pairs with N40 (Oyima-Antseleve, expectations gap) and N49 (Martins et al., firm characteristics) to form a comprehensive "market reaction dynamics" cluster for H1, H3, H5.

### Article N51 — NEW ⭐
**Rickett, L.K. (2026).** Major cybersecurity breaches: Shaping corporate cybersecurity policies and closing the gaps. *Journal of Corporate Accounting & Finance*, Vol. 37.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1002/jcaf.70032
**Wiley Online Library URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jcaf.70032
**Published:** Journal of Corporate Accounting & Finance (Wiley), 2026.

**Summary:** This conceptual and case-based paper examines how major cybersecurity incidents (including the ChoicePoint breach and subsequent cases) have shaped corporate cybersecurity policies and governance structures over the past two decades, using routine activity theory and related criminological frameworks to explain the persistence of cybercrime despite increasing regulatory requirements. The paper documents the evolution of cybersecurity from an IT function to a board-level strategic imperative — with the CISO increasingly positioned as a C-suite officer responsible for integrating security into business planning — and examines how mandatory disclosure requirements have accelerated this governance elevation. AI-related cybersecurity risks and the limitations of current frameworks in addressing them are also addressed.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** The CISO-as-C-suite elevation is a governance mechanism directly relevant to H6's post-breach accountability chain: as CISO roles become more prominent and board-visible, CISO turnover (not just CEO/CIO) following significant breaches is an increasingly important H6 outcome. This paper contextualizes why the CISO-specific turnover gap in the literature (noted in the July 27 coverage assessment) is growing in real-world significance.
- **H2 (Regulation → governance response):** Mandatory disclosure requirements (FCC/SEC) are documented as catalysts for governance elevation — directly linking regulatory design to the organizational accountability structures that H2 predicts will change post-mandate.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** The gap between firms with mature CISO structures and those without is a governance-quality firm-characteristic dimension relevant to H1's cross-sectional moderation.
- **NOTE:** Primarily conceptual/case-based rather than large-sample empirical. Use as contextual framing and institutional background for H6 rather than as an empirical test. The ChoicePoint case analysis provides historical perspective on how disclosure regulation originated in practice.

**Published:** Elsevier, accepted/published October 2025.

**Summary:** Examining whether board-level cybersecurity governance mechanisms improve the quality of cybersecurity risk disclosures in 10-K filings under the SEC's 2023 mandatory disclosure rule, this paper documents that independent board members significantly increase cybersecurity disclosure quality and quantity — acting as an effective governance and oversight mechanism. The study fills a prior research gap by directly testing the SEC's presumed link between board cybersecurity expertise and improved disclosure outcomes. Results have implications for how governance quality moderates the effectiveness of mandatory disclosure mandates.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** ⭐⭐ Board independence is a governance-quality firm characteristic that modifies disclosure behavior under mandatory requirements — a direct H1 cross-sectional moderator. Firms with stronger board governance produce higher-quality disclosures, which prior literature links to attenuated market reactions (the legitimacy-theory mechanism from N42).
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** The finding that board independence improves disclosure quality under the SEC mandate provides H2 with a governance-quality heterogeneity mechanism: firms with weaker boards comply minimally, producing disclosures that may still generate residual information asymmetry and stock return impacts.
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** Board independence is an antecedent of post-breach executive accountability — independent directors are more likely to pursue managerial accountability following breaches, connecting this paper to H6's governance-quality moderation.
- **NOTE:** Authors TBC — confirm full author names and journal title via ScienceDirect page before dissertation citation. The pii pattern (S0278-4254-25-00095-X) is consistent with the *International Journal of Accounting Information Systems* or a related Elsevier journal from 2025. The board independence finding complements Haapamäki & Sihvonen (N48) on firm characteristics under the same SEC mandatory regime — together these two papers constitute a "mandatory-regime governance" pair.

### Article N53 — NEW ⭐ [INTERNATIONAL COMPARISON FLAG]
**Lunga, T. & Kirsten, E. (2026).** Cybersecurity risk disclosure practices of Johannesburg Stock Exchange-listed companies. *International Journal of Disclosure and Governance*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1057/s41310-026-00389-5
**Springer Link URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41310-026-00389-5
**Published:** International Journal of Disclosure and Governance (Springer/Palgrave Macmillan), 2026.

**Summary:** Using manual content analysis of the 100 largest capitalised companies on the Johannesburg Stock Exchange, this paper examines the extent and quality of cybersecurity disclosures in annual reports, finding that both industry classification and prior attack experience significantly influence disclosure behavior. Quality is heterogeneous: high-disclosure-volume firms do not necessarily produce higher-quality disclosures. The results provide evidence that voluntary disclosure norms in an emerging market context differ substantially from mandatory disclosure regimes, and that industry-level breach exposure is a key driver of disclosure decisions even in the absence of prescriptive requirements.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Industry and attack history are H1-consistent determinants of disclosure behavior — the emerging-market context shows that these firm-characteristic effects are robust across regulatory environments, strengthening H1's generalizability claims.
- **H4 (Breach type):** Industry classification as a disclosure driver is a proxy for breach-type exposure (financial firms face payment-data risks; healthcare firms face PHI risks) — an H4-adjacent finding about how sector-specific breach risk shapes disclosure quality.
- **NOTE:** ⭐ *Emerging market (South Africa) sample — lower direct relevance to U.S. FCC/SEC dissertation context.* Use as international comparison evidence, particularly for dissertation sections arguing that the FCC's mandatory approach is superior to voluntary-disclosure regimes (as documented in JSE context). Flag as international robustness/comparison, not as a primary U.S.-market finding. One star for lower direct relevance despite publication recency.

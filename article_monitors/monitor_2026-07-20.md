### Article N32 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Haapamäki, E. & Sihvonen, J. (2026).** Mandatory cybersecurity disclosure: Early evidence from 10-K reports. *International Journal of Accounting Information Systems*, *57*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.accinf.2026.100XXX *(verify exact article number at ScienceDirect pii: S1467089526000059)*
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1467089526000059
**Published:** International Journal of Accounting Information Systems (Elsevier), Vol. 57, 2026

**Summary:** Using textual analysis of 3,440 Item 1C disclosures in 10-K filings from 2024 (the first year after the SEC's 2023 mandatory cybersecurity disclosure rule took effect), this paper provides the earliest large-sample evidence on how U.S. public companies have responded to the new annual governance-disclosure mandate. Firms largely complied, but disclosure characteristics vary significantly by firm size, financial performance, auditor quality, cybersecurity and litigation risk exposures, and peer practices. Critically, factors such as past cyber incidents, IT weaknesses, firm digitalization, or technology-sector status show no significant influence on the length or content of these annual governance disclosures — suggesting the mandatory rule has reduced the signal value of voluntary disclosure variation.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (FCC/SEC regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ The most recent empirical evaluation of the SEC's 2023 mandatory cybersecurity disclosure rule using a large sample (3,440 10-Ks). The finding that prior breach history does *not* drive disclosure content under the mandate challenges the assumption that mandatory disclosure automatically increases informativeness — directly relevant to H2's causal identification of regulatory impact on market outcomes.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** Firm-size and auditor-quality determinants of disclosure depth provide cross-sectional variation in disclosure quality under the new regime — a key H1 moderator. Larger and Big 4-audited firms disclose more, potentially reducing information asymmetry selectively across the size distribution.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** If mandatory disclosure doesn't improve content for prior-breach firms, information asymmetry reduction may be uneven — inconsistent with the uniform H5 reduction hypothesis. Pair with Block (2025, N28) to triangulate the new SEC rule's market-microstructure effects.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *First large-sample empirical study of SEC mandatory cybersecurity disclosure in 10-K filings.* Published in 2026 using first-year data — treat as preliminary but high-priority for H2's regulatory-design chapter. Monitor for follow-up revisions as more years accumulate.

### Article N33 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Gordon, L.A., Loeb, M.P., Zhou, L. & Wilford, A.L. (2024).** Empirical evidence on disclosing cyber breaches in an 8-K report: Initial exploratory evidence. *Journal of Accounting and Public Policy*, 2024.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaccpubpol.2024.107XXX *(verify exact article number at ScienceDirect pii: S0278425424000498)*
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0278425424000498
**Published:** Journal of Accounting and Public Policy (Elsevier), 2024

**Summary:** Gordon, Loeb, Zhou & Wilford — pioneers of the Gordon-Loeb cybersecurity investment model — examine whether the *channel* of breach disclosure (Form 8-K vs. alternative sources) explains the contradictory findings in prior market-reaction studies. Using a sample of 670 U.S. public-firm cyber breaches, they find that breaches disclosed via Form 8-K trigger significantly stronger *immediate* negative stock price reactions than breaches disclosed through alternative channels (e.g., media reports, company websites). However, the 8-K-disclosed firms also exhibit a more pronounced post-event *recovery*, suggesting the mandatory formal disclosure channel accelerates price discovery and reduces post-announcement information asymmetry. The post-announcement stock market reaction to 8-K disclosure is positive and statistically significant, while alternative-channel-disclosed breaches show a persistent negative drift.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (FCC/SEC regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ Directly addresses H2: mandatory Form 8-K disclosure produces a distinctive return pattern (stronger initial drop, faster recovery) relative to voluntary/alternative disclosure. Provides empirical foundation for H2's prediction that the FCC's 2024 mandatory reporting rule will reshape the timing and magnitude of market reactions for telecom carriers. The disclosure-channel comparison is the cleanest available test of disclosure-mandate effects on return dynamics.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** The faster post-event recovery for 8-K filers is consistent with H5's prediction that mandatory disclosure reduces post-announcement information asymmetry, enabling faster price discovery. This is the Gordon-Loeb group's first market-microstructure result.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** The disclosure-channel choice (8-K vs. alternative) is endogenous and correlated with firm characteristics — the cross-sectional determinants of channel choice provide H1-relevant moderators.
- **H4 (Breach type):** The sample of 670 breaches likely includes variation in breach type that could be analyzed for H4-style channel × breach-type interactions.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Gordon & Loeb authorship makes this a must-cite for H2 — their methodological credibility anchors the disclosure-channel comparison.* The 8-K vs. alternative-channel framework can be directly extended to the FCC 2024 mandatory rule context (telecom carriers previously had no 8-K obligation for cyber breaches).

### Article N34 — NEW ⭐
**Gao, L. et al. (2025).** Cybersecurity risk governance and companies' cybersecurity risk disclosures in their 10-K filings. *Journal of Accounting and Public Policy*, 2025.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaccpubpol.2025.107XXX *(verify at ScienceDirect pii: S027842542500095X; confirm all authors)*
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S027842542500095X
**Published:** Journal of Accounting and Public Policy (Elsevier), 2025

**Summary:** Investigates how internal cybersecurity governance structures — board-level cyber expertise, CISO presence, audit committee oversight — drive the content and quality of cybersecurity risk disclosures in annual 10-K reports. The study finds that stronger cybersecurity governance is associated with more substantive and consistent disclosures, with regulatory changes (SEC 2023 rules) acting as an external catalyst for disclosure standardization. The paper complements Haapamäki & Sihvonen (2026, N32) by examining the *governance determinants* of disclosure content rather than firm-level financial characteristics.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** SEC regulatory changes drive disclosure standardization — provides a governance-quality mechanism through which H2's regulatory treatment effects flow. Firms with stronger cybersecurity governance comply more substantively, potentially generating differential market reactions.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Board-level cyber expertise and CISO presence are firm-characteristic governance variables directly relevant to H1's cross-sectional heterogeneity prediction.
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** CISO presence and board-level governance are antecedents to the executive accountability framework underlying H6 — governance quality shapes both disclosure behavior and post-breach accountability pressures.
- **NOTE:** Confirm all author names via publisher page before final citation. The lead author "Gao, L." is referenced from secondary source.

### Article N35 — NEW ⭐
**Martins, A.M. & Moutinho, N. (2025).** Stock-term market impact of major cyber-attacks: Evidence for the ten most exposed insurance firms to cyber risk. *Finance Research Letters*, *71*(C).
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.frl.2024.106361
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1544612324013904
**Published:** Finance Research Letters (Elsevier), Vol. 71(C), 2025

**Summary:** Using event-study methodology, the paper examines the short-term stock market impact of major cyberattacks on the ten U.S. insurance firms most exposed to cyber risk (as measured by their cyber insurance portfolio concentration). Results document significantly negative abnormal returns for these highly exposed insurance firms around cyberattack disclosure dates, with effects amplified for small insurers and breaches involving financial information loss. This is a companion piece to Martins, Moutinho & Cró (2025, N20), which studied breached firms and cybersecurity-sector firms; here the focus shifts to a third affected party — the cyber-risk insurance market.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** Firm size is confirmed as the dominant cross-sectional moderator of negative reactions within the insurance sector, consistent with the H1 size-moderation hypothesis. The specialty-exposure measure (cyber portfolio concentration) provides a novel proxy for cybersecurity risk that could inform H1's heterogeneity analysis.
- **H4 (Breach type):** Financial-information breaches produce the most severe market reactions for exposed insurers — consistent with H4's prediction that financial data breaches carry the highest market-impact severity across affected parties beyond just the breached firm.
- **H5 (Volatility / systemic spillovers):** The insurance-sector spillover confirms that breach events create information asymmetry and volatility across parties beyond the breached firm — extending H5's scope to third-party market participants.
- **NOTE:** Same first two authors as N20 (Martins, Moutinho & Cró, 2025); this is a companion paper studying a different affected population (insurance firms vs. breached firms). Cite both to triangulate spillover effects.

### Article N36 — NEW ⭐
**Liang, Z., Xu, S., Chen, D.J., Li, J. & Liu, B. (2024).** From Departure to Breach: The Impact of IT Employee Turnover on Cybersecurity Risks. *ICIS 2024 Proceedings* (Track: Security, Privacy, and IS Risk).
**DOI:** N/A (conference proceedings)
**URL:** https://aisel.aisnet.org/icis2024/security/security/9/
**Presented:** International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS) 2024

**Summary:** Examines whether the departure of employees engaged in IT-related tasks — across all organizational hierarchy levels, not just executives — increases a firm's probability of experiencing a data breach. Using firm-level panel data, the study finds a significant positive correlation between IT employee turnover and subsequent breach likelihood. Notably, recruiting new IT employees does not mitigate the negative impact of departures, suggesting knowledge loss and institutional memory gaps (not headcount gaps) are the primary mechanism. The finding isolates workforce-continuity cybersecurity risk as distinct from executive-governance risk.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** The reverse causal channel — IT *employee* (not executive) turnover → breach risk — complements H6's breach → executive turnover direction. Together they form a governance feedback loop: IT departures create breach vulnerability; breaches trigger executive accountability. Cite alongside N23 (Hartmann & Carmenate, 2021) which covers the executive direction.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** The finding that new-hire mitigation fails implies that organizational knowledge depth (not just staffing level) is the relevant firm characteristic — a novel H1 moderator for firms with high IT workforce churn.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** Knowledge gaps from IT turnover are a form of *internal* information asymmetry between management and security capabilities — could inform H5's pre-breach vulnerability channel.
- **NOTE:** ICIS 2024 conference paper; check for published journal version and update citation before dissertation submission.

### Article N37 — NEW ⭐
**Rezaee, Z., Zhou, T. & Bu, D. (2024).** Corporate social irresponsibility and the occurrence of data breaches: A stakeholder management perspective. *International Journal of Accounting Information Systems*, *53*(C).
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.accinf.2024.100697 *(verify at ScienceDirect pii: S1467089524000101)*
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1467089524000101
**Published:** International Journal of Accounting Information Systems (Elsevier), Vol. 53(C), 2024

**Summary:** From a stakeholder management perspective, this paper examines how corporate social irresponsibility (CSI) increases the likelihood of data breach occurrence. The central finding is that CSI activities that violate broader social norms (e.g., environmental harm, labor violations) attract hack-activist targeting, while CSI activities that directly harm customers (product deficiencies, fraud, privacy violations) motivate internal breaches and disgruntled-employee attacks. The interaction of CSR engagement and CSI practices creates a non-linear relationship: CSR can *heighten* breach risk when both CSR and CSI relate to the same economic value delivery dimension, generating stakeholder confusion and reputational fragility.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** Firms with persistent CSI records face escalating hack-activist targeting — each breach increases the reputational damage from subsequent incidents. This provides a stakeholder-theory mechanism for H3's repeat-breach market penalty.
- **H4 (Breach type):** The distinction between external hack-activist attacks (driven by environmental/social CSI) and internal breaches (driven by product/customer CSI) maps directly onto H4's breach-type taxonomy — CSI-driven breach types have distinct market-impact signatures.
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** CSI governance failures that produce breaches create accountability pressure across both board-level governance and executive leadership — connecting H6 to the corporate governance quality literature.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** CSI exposure level and CSR engagement are firm-characteristic cross-sectional moderators consistent with H1's heterogeneity framework.

### Article N38 — NEW ⭐
**Xu, J., Yue, W.T., Leung, A.C.M. & Su, Q. (2024).** Focusing on the fundamentals? An investigation of the relationship between corporate social irresponsibility and data breach risk. *Decision Support Systems*, *182*, Article 114252.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dss.2024.114252
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S016792362400085X
**Published:** Decision Support Systems (Elsevier), Vol. 182, 2024

**Summary:** Empirical analysis of data breaches in publicly listed U.S. firms from 2005 to 2017 examines the relative importance of different CSI dimensions in driving breach risk. Key finding: CSI activities that jeopardize a company's economic value delivery (product deficiencies, service failures, customer harm) dominate environmental CSI and broader social-norm violations in predicting breach occurrence. The paper also finds that CSR investments have a dual moderating effect — CSR generally reduces CSI-induced breach risk, but when both CSR and CSI relate to economic value delivery, CSR *increases* breach risk by signaling inconsistency to stakeholders. The City University of Hong Kong team uses an extensive CSI taxonomy from KLD STATS data linked to the Privacy Rights Clearinghouse breach database.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H4 (Breach type):** The CSI-driven breach-type analysis provides a governance-quality antecedent to the breach-type market-impact taxonomy in H4. Firms with economic-value-delivery CSI are more likely to experience financial-data breaches, which carry the highest H4 market penalties.
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** Persistent economic-value-delivery CSI compounds breach risk over time — a firm-quality mechanism for H3's escalating repeat-breach penalty. Firms with deep CSI problems should face higher repeat-breach probabilities and therefore steeper market penalties.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** CSR engagement level and CSI dimension as firm-characteristic moderators align with H1's governance-quality and sector-risk channels.
- **NOTE:** The 2005–2017 sample period predates major disclosure mandates (SEC 2023, FCC 2024); the breach-risk determinants may differ in the mandatory-disclosure era. Flag as a pre-regulation baseline when citing in H2 context. Pair with N37 (Rezaee et al., 2024) — both use similar stakeholder and governance frameworks but with distinct mechanisms and datasets.

### Article N39 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Ho, K.C., Lee, S.C., Pan, Z. & Karathanasopoulos, A. (2026).** How does cyber risk impact systemic stability? *Journal of Forecasting*, *45*(2), 589–604.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1002/for.70032
**URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/for.70032
**Published:** Journal of Forecasting (Wiley), Vol. 45, Issue 2, March 2026

**Summary:** Using machine learning techniques applied to firm-level data from 2006 to 2018, the paper constructs a novel cyber risk measure and investigates its relationship to systemic financial stability. The core finding is that higher firm-level cyber risk is significantly associated with increased systemic risk (proxied by asset-return correlations across firms), with the cyber-risk-driven systemic component carrying a significant risk premium in the cross-section of future stock returns. The results are robust to exclusion of financial firms and the 2008–2009 financial crisis period. This paper is the first to document a *systemic* channel from firm-level cyber risk to cross-market return co-movement, establishing that data breaches and cyber vulnerabilities are not idiosyncratic events but systemic financial risks.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** ⭐⭐ The systemic-stability mechanism extends H5 beyond individual-firm volatility: cyber risk propagates correlated volatility across firms (elevated asset-return correlations), creating market-wide information asymmetry during breach-disclosure periods. The risk premium finding confirms that investors price this systemic component — a novel empirical foundation for H5's aggregate market-impact claim.
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** Mandatory disclosure rules (FCC/SEC) reduce information asymmetry about individual firms' cyber risk; if cyber risk is systemic, disclosure-driven repricing of one firm affects correlated peers — providing a transmission mechanism for H2's regulatory spillover effects.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** The cross-section of cyber risk exposure varies by firm characteristics — size, leverage, sector, and digitalization — generating heterogeneous systemic contributions consistent with H1's moderation framework.
- **H4 (Breach type):** Financial-data breaches would carry the highest systemic risk contributions under this framework (financial information loss increases correlated pricing errors across banks, insurers, and tech firms simultaneously).
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Published in 2026 — the most recent journal article added to this monitor.* The systemic-risk channel is a novel theoretical contribution for the dissertation's Chapter 2 (Literature Review) — extends H5 from microstructure to macroprudential territory. DOI confirmed at Wiley Online Library.

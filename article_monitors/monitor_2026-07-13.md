### Article N24 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Liu, C. & Babar, M.A. (2026).** Corporate cybersecurity risk and data breaches: A systematic review of empirical research. *Australian Journal of Management*, *51*(1), 62–92.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1177/03128962241293658
**URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03128962241293658
**Published:** Australian Journal of Management (SAGE), Vol. 51(1), February 2026

**Summary:** A landmark systematic review of 203 empirical studies across 12 disciplines mapping the determinants and consequences of corporate cybersecurity risk. The authors synthesize findings on how breaches affect stock prices, volatility, bid-ask spreads, trading volumes, and information asymmetry, and develop a novel multi-disciplinary framework linking breach drivers, attack impacts, and feedback mechanisms. The review documents that breach announcements prompt markets to reassess cybersecurity risk, significantly increasing stock price volatility, risk, trading volume, liquidity, and information asymmetry — and that the magnitude of these effects varies systematically with firm characteristics and breach severity.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** ⭐⭐ The cross-disciplinary synthesis explicitly maps how firm characteristics (size, governance, industry, financial constraints) moderate breach market impacts — the most comprehensive evidence base available for H1's cross-sectional moderation hypotheses.
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** ⭐⭐ The review's core finding — that breaches increase stock volatility, bid-ask spreads, and trading volume — directly corroborates H5. Provides 203-study-backed meta-evidence for H5 mechanisms.
- **H2 (Regulation impact):** Synthesizes evidence on how regulatory environments (U.S. state DBN laws, GDPR, HIPAA/HITECH, SEC rules) condition market responses — a direct reference for H2's cross-regulatory comparative analysis.
- **H4 (Breach type):** Multi-disciplinary synthesis covers differential market reactions by breach type (financial, health, credential, operational) across all disciplines — critical for H4's taxonomy.
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** Reviews governance-response literature including executive accountability and board-level risk management changes post-breach.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Highest-priority reference added this week.* This review should be cited in the dissertation's literature review as the authoritative comprehensive synthesis. Its 203-study scope makes it both a gap-map and a methods benchmark.

### Article N25 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Chen, X., Hilary, G. & Tian, X. (2024).** Mandatory data breach disclosure and insider trading. *Journal of Business Finance & Accounting*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/jbfa.12842
**URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jbfa.12842
**Published:** Journal of Business Finance & Accounting (Wiley), 2024

**Summary:** Examines whether state-level mandatory data breach disclosure laws affect insider-selling behavior. The study finds that insiders' selling profits are *greater* after states adopt mandatory disclosure laws — consistent with insiders personally hedging cybersecurity risks in the capital market when laws require public disclosure but enforcement is weak. The effect is stronger for firms with weaker corporate governance, higher cybersecurity risk, and no prior investment in material cyber protection programs. Insiders profit less in states with stricter laws and higher litigation risk, suggesting legal deterrence moderates the opportunistic trading channel.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** ⭐⭐ The insider trading channel is a direct manifestation of information asymmetry — insiders exploit private knowledge of breach severity before mandatory disclosures are processed by the market. Provides a novel H5 mechanism linking disclosure mandates to market microstructure outcomes.
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ Demonstrates that mandatory disclosure regulation produces heterogeneous outcomes — reducing information asymmetry for well-enforced laws but exacerbating it where enforcement is weak. Directly relevant to H2's causal identification design.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Governance quality, cybersecurity investment history, and litigation environment are cross-sectional moderators — all H1-relevant firm characteristics.
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** Insiders at firms with higher prior cybersecurity risk (consistent with repeat exposure) trade more aggressively, suggesting compounding insider-trading penalties mirror H3's market-penalty escalation.
- **NOTE:** Pair with N19 (Obaydin et al., 2024, JBFA) — both are JBFA 2024 papers examining unintended consequences of mandatory disclosure laws; N19 addresses bad-news hoarding/crash risk, N25 addresses insider trading. Together they frame the perverse-incentives literature for H2.

### Article N26 — NEW ⭐
**Akyildirim, E., Conlon, T., Corbet, S. & Hou, G. (2024).** HACKED: Understanding the stock market response to cyberattacks. *Journal of International Financial Markets, Institutions and Money*, *97*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intfin.2024.101975 *(verify at ScienceDirect pii: S1042443124001483)*
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1042443124001483
**Published:** Journal of International Financial Markets, Institutions and Money (Elsevier), Vol. 97, 2024

**Summary:** Using a large international sample, the study finds that stock returns decrease by −0.24% on the day after a cyberattack disclosure, with the effect reversing within approximately two weeks, suggesting temporary market overreaction. Critically, the negative reaction is largest for firms experiencing *reoccurring* cyber events and for the most severe breaches. The magnitude of stock decline is not explained by national macroeconomic or institutional factors — it is driven by company-specific characteristics including size, volatility, credit ranking, and asset volatility. The study emphasizes that these findings strengthen the case for cyber risk disclosure requirements.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** ⭐⭐ The finding that repeat-incident firms suffer the largest market declines is the strongest cross-country corroboration of H3 yet identified. The reoccurring-event amplification is central to H3's escalating-penalty hypothesis.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Company-specific factors — size, volatility, credit quality, asset volatility — explain cross-sectional variation in market reactions, consistent with H1's moderation hypotheses.
- **H4 (Breach severity as type proxy):** Severity-conditioned market reactions provide international evidence for H4's differential response across attack types.
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** The short-lived negative reaction followed by reversal is consistent with temporary volatility spikes and information processing lags documented under H5.
- **H2 (Regulatory advocacy):** The paper's explicit advocacy for cyber risk disclosure requirements provides a policy-context anchor for H2's regulatory design discussion.
- **FLAG:** International sample (not U.S.-only) adds generalizability beyond the U.S.-centric prior literature — an important robustness point for the dissertation.

### Article N27 — NEW ⭐⭐
**Cao, Z., Phan, H.V. & Silveri, S. (2024).** Data breach disclosures and stock price crash risk: Evidence from data breach notification laws. *International Review of Financial Analysis*, *93*, 103164.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.irfa.2024.103164
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1057521924000966
**Published:** International Review of Financial Analysis (Elsevier), Vol. 93, 2024

**Summary:** Exploiting the staggered adoption of U.S. state data breach notification (DBN) laws as a quasi-natural experiment, this paper shows that DBN laws *increase* future stock price crash risk. The mechanism is investor concern amplification: mandatory disclosure heightens investors' awareness of breach consequences and firm data-security vulnerability, paradoxically increasing downside tail risk. The positive relation between DBN laws and crash risk is most pronounced for firms with weaker corporate governance, higher financial constraints, and higher information asymmetry. The paper complements Obaydin et al. (2024, JBFA) which attributes crash risk to managerial hoarding; here the authors attribute it to elevated investor anxiety post-disclosure.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (Regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ Provides causal evidence (staggered quasi-experiment) that mandatory disclosure regulation increases crash risk — a specific form of adverse stock return outcome central to H2. Paired with Obaydin et al. (N19), the two 2024 papers offer complementary mechanisms: hoarding vs. investor-anxiety channels.
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** The crash-risk increase is most pronounced in high-information-asymmetry firms — a direct H5 channel connecting disclosure mandates to extreme volatility outcomes.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Governance quality and financial constraints moderate the regulatory impact — confirms H1's cross-sectional heterogeneity prediction.
- **H3 (Repeat breach):** The investor-anxiety channel is likely amplified for repeat-breach firms where investors have accumulated negative priors, consistent with H3.
- **FLAG:** ⭐⭐ *Critical H2 paper — pair with N19 (Obaydin et al., 2024), N25 (Chen et al., 2024), and N28 (Block, 2025) to build the complete regulatory-impact literature framework for H2.*

### Article N28 — NEW ⭐
**Block, M. (2025).** Market reactions to material cybersecurity incident disclosures. *arXiv preprint* arXiv:2512.06144.
**DOI:** N/A (working paper)
**URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06144
**Posted:** November 2025; Johns Hopkins University

**Summary:** Examines short-term market reactions to material cybersecurity incidents disclosed under Item 1.05 of SEC Form 8-K — the first empirical study of the SEC's 2023 mandatory cybersecurity disclosure rule. Using 54 Form 8-K filings (Item 1.05) from 36 unique companies filed through November 2025, the study finds negative average price reactions post-disclosure but with notably muted magnitude (approximately −0.7% one day post-8-K, −2.1% after five days). Smaller companies experience more pronounced declines; sector and beta differences are not statistically significant in the early sample. The muted aggregate reaction may reflect the market treating cyber incidents as routine operational risk under the new disclosure regime.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (FCC/SEC regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ The most direct empirical test available for H2 using the SEC's December 2023 mandatory cyber disclosure rule. The finding of muted market reaction challenges the hypothesis that mandatory disclosure uniformly amplifies negative returns — suggesting the market may price in disclosure-regime uncertainty before formal filings.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** Size heterogeneity (smaller firms suffer larger declines) confirms H1's firm-size moderation within the new mandatory-disclosure regime.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** The muted aggregate reaction may reflect reduced information asymmetry under mandatory disclosure — investors are less surprised by cyber events when firms must rapidly file Form 8-K. This aligns with H5's prediction that better disclosure reduces market microstructure costs.
- **CAVEAT:** Small sample (54 8-K filings as of Nov. 2025) limits statistical power; treat as preliminary evidence. Monitor for published version.
- **NOTE:** This is a working paper. Include with appropriate caveats and check for publication by dissertation submission date.

### Article N29 — NEW ⭐
**Wu, C., Ma, T. & Zhang, J. (2025).** Impact of cybersecurity disclosure frequency on stock price crash risk. *Journal of Corporate Accounting & Finance*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1002/jcaf.22775
**URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jcaf.22775
**Published:** Journal of Corporate Accounting & Finance (Wiley), 2025

**Summary:** Examines how the *frequency* of cybersecurity disclosures in annual reports (10-K filings) relates to stock price crash risk, finding that more frequent cybersecurity disclosures correlate with higher crash risk. The mechanism proposed is that high disclosure frequency signals elevated cybersecurity exposure, increasing investors' perceived downside tail risk. Results complement crash-risk evidence from disclosure-mandate studies by examining voluntary disclosure quantity rather than regulatory thresholds.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H5 (Volatility / information asymmetry):** Disclosure frequency as a driver of crash risk provides a novel H5 mechanism linking disclosure behavior to extreme downside volatility outcomes — extendable to the FCC/SEC mandatory disclosure context.
- **H2 (Regulation → returns):** Mandatory disclosure rules effectively increase the floor on cybersecurity disclosure frequency — if higher frequency raises crash risk, mandatory rules may have unintended downside tail effects, directly relevant to H2's causal hypotheses.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Disclosure frequency likely varies by firm size, governance quality, and industry — all H1-relevant moderators.
- **NOTE:** Journal of Corporate Accounting & Finance (Wiley) is a practitioner-oriented accounting journal; weight accordingly relative to top-tier outlets cited elsewhere in this monitor.

### Article N30 — NEW ⭐
**López, F.A., Jara-Sarrúa, L. & Morales-Parada, F. (2025).** Cybersecurity disclosure in the financial sector: An examination of the influence of incident exposure, governance practices, and regulatory context. *Electronic Commerce Research*.
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10660-025-10081-5
**URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10660-025-10081-5
**Published:** Electronic Commerce Research (Springer Nature), 2025

**Summary:** Using legitimacy theory, the study investigates what drives cybersecurity disclosure behavior among banks, focusing on three dimensions: (1) incident exposure (actual prior breach history), (2) governance practices (board composition, audit committee), and (3) regulatory context (jurisdiction-specific disclosure mandates). Banks that have experienced more cybersecurity incidents and operate under stricter regulatory regimes disclose more cybersecurity information in their annual reports. Governance quality moderates the relationship between incident exposure and disclosure quantity. The financial-sector focus and bank-specific institutional context make this a direct comparison point for any FCC-regulated carrier analysis under H2.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H2 (FCC/SEC regulation → stock returns):** ⭐⭐ Provides direct evidence that regulatory context drives disclosure behavior in the financial sector — foundational for H2's regulatory-treatment design. The FCC's 2024 rules for telecom carriers parallel the banking regulatory context studied here.
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** Prior incident exposure drives more disclosure — consistent with H3's repeat-breach escalation hypothesis; repeat-breach firms face heightened regulatory pressure to disclose more, potentially amplifying market reactions.
- **H6 (Executive turnover):** Governance mechanisms (board composition, audit committees) that drive disclosure also moderate accountability — relevant to H6's board-pressure mechanism for executive turnover.
- **H1 (Firm characteristics):** Governance quality and regulatory jurisdiction as cross-sectional moderators align with H1's firm-characteristic heterogeneity.

### Article N31 — NEW ⭐
**Oyima-Antseleve, N. (2025).** Expectations and market reactions to data breaches. *Finance Research Letters* (forthcoming / ScienceDirect advance).
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finlet.2025.106243 *(verify at ScienceDirect pii: S154461232502450X)*
**URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S154461232502450X
**SSRN preprint:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5240536
**Posted/Published:** May 2025; California State Polytechnic University, San Luis Obispo

**Summary:** Proposes and tests that stock price reactions to data breach announcements are significantly explained by shareholders' *prior expectations* regarding breach scale and magnitude. Using breach data from 2005 to 2019, the study finds that the magnitude of subsequent sales declines in breached firms aligns with observed stock price fluctuations around announcements — suggesting markets are forward-looking and condition reactions on pre-existing beliefs about breach severity. Firms that outperform expectations (i.e., breaches are less severe than anticipated) experience smaller negative reactions, providing an expectations-adjustment framework for understanding cross-sectional variation in CARs.

**Relevance Analysis:**
- **H1 (Firm characteristics → market pricing):** The expectations-adjustment framework provides a behavioral mechanism for H1's cross-sectional variation: firms with higher prior cybersecurity-risk expectations face smaller incremental negative reactions for a given breach. This moderates firm-characteristic effects documented elsewhere.
- **H3 (Repeat breach penalty):** Prior breach history shapes investor expectations — repeat-breach firms face more negative reactions because expectations of low cybersecurity quality lower the threshold for negative surprise. A direct theoretical mechanism for H3.
- **H5 (Information asymmetry):** Information about breach scale and pre-existing market expectations interacts with information asymmetry — markets with better prior signals about firm cybersecurity quality (lower asymmetry) exhibit smaller reactions consistent with expectations.
- **H4 (Breach type):** Expectations about breach severity likely vary by breach type — financial breaches carry higher expected severity, amplifying reactions consistent with H4.
- **CAVEAT:** Working paper / advance article; verify journal assignment and DOI before final citation.
